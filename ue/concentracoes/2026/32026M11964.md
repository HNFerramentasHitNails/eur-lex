5.       COMPETITIVE ASSESSMENT

5.1.     Introduction
(102)    Under Article 2(2) and (3) of the Merger Regulation, the Commission must assess
         whether a proposed concentration would significantly impede effective
         competition in the internal market or in a substantial part of it, in particular
         through the creation or strengthening of a dominant position.
(103)    In this respect, a merger may entail horizontal and/or non-horizontal (namely,
         vertical or conglomerate) effects. Horizontal effects are those deriving from a
         concentration where the undertakings concerned are actual or potential
         competitors of each other in one or more of the relevant markets concerned.
         Vertical effects are those deriving from a concentration where the undertakings
         concerned are active on different levels of the supply chain. Conglomerate effects
         are those deriving from a concentration where the undertakings concerned are in
         a relationship which is neither horizontal nor vertical. A concentration may
         involve all three types of effects. In such a case, the Commission will appraise
         horizontal and non-horizontal effects in accordance with the guidance set out in
         the relevant notices, that is to say the Horizontal Merger Guidelines (81) and the
         Non-Horizontal Merger Guidelines. (82)

5.4.       Horizontal effects

        5.4.1.   Legal framework
(174)      The Horizontal Merger Guidelines (124) describe two main ways in which
           horizontal mergers may significantly impede effective competition, in particular,
           by creating or strengthening a dominant position: (i) by eliminating important
           competitive constraints on one or more firms, which consequently would have
           increased market power, without resorting to coordinated behaviour (non-
           coordinated effects); or (ii) by changing the nature of competition in such a way
           that firms that previously were not coordinating their behaviour, are significantly
           more likely to coordinate and raise prices or otherwise harm effective competition
           (coordinated effects).
(175)      A merger giving rise to horizontal non-coordinated effects might significantly
           impede effective competition by creating or strengthening the dominant position
           of a single firm, one which, typically, would have an appreciably larger market
           share than the next competitor post-merger. Moreover, also mergers that do not
           lead to the creation of or the strengthening of a single firm’s dominant position
           may create competition concerns under the substantive test set out in Article 2(2)
           and Article 2(3) of the Merger Regulation. Regarding mergers in oligopolistic
           markets, the Merger Regulation clarifies that ‘under certain circumstances,
           concentrations involving the elimination of important competitive constraints that
           the merging parties exerted upon each other, as well as a reduction of competitive
           pressure on the remaining competitors, may, even in the absence of a likelihood
           of coordination between the members of the oligopoly, result in a significant
           impediment to effective competition.’ (125)
(176)      The Horizontal Merger Guidelines list a number of factors which may influence
           whether or not significant horizontal non-coordinated effects are likely to result
           from a merger, such as the large market shares of the merging firms, the fact that
           the merging firms are close competitors, the limited possibilities for customers to
           switch suppliers, or the fact that the merger would eliminate an important
           competitive force. Not all those factors need to be present to make significant
           non-coordinated effects likely and it is not an exhaustive list. (126)

(229)      According to the Non-Horizontal Guidelines, (227) non-coordinated effects may
           significantly impede effective competition as a result of a vertical merger if such
           merger gives rise to foreclosure. Foreclosure occurs where actual or potential
           competitors' access to supplies or markets is hampered or eliminated as a result
           of the merger, thereby reducing those companies' ability and/or incentive to
           compete. (228) Such foreclosure may discourage entry or expansion of competitors
           or encourage their exit. (229)
(230)      The Non-Horizontal Guidelines distinguish between two forms of foreclosure.
           First, input foreclosure occurs where the merger is likely to raise the costs of
           downstream competitors by restricting their access to an important input. (230)
           Foreclosure may also take more subtle forms, such as the degradation of the
           quality of input supplied. In its assessment, the Commission may consider a series
           of alternative or complementary possible strategies. (231) Second, customer
           foreclosure occurs where the merger is likely to foreclose upstream competitors
           by restricting their access to a sufficient customer base. (232)
(231)      In assessing the likelihood of an anticompetitive foreclosure scenario, the
           Commission examines, first, whether the merged entity would have, post-merger,
           the ability to substantially foreclose access to inputs or customers; second,
           whether it would have the incentive to do so; and third, whether a foreclosure
           strategy would have a significant detrimental effect on competition. In practice,
           these factors are often examined together as they are closely intertwined. (233)

5.5.4.3.1.1.1. Infrastructure and Security Metadata
(267)    The Commission considers that the Infrastructure and Security Metadata is
         unlikely to constitute commercially sensitive data within the meaning of

(268)    First, the Infrastructure and Security Metadata does not constitute commercially
         sensitive information in view of its nature. On the one hand, through Infrastructure
         and Security Metadata, Wiz has no access to a cloud infrastructure provider’s

         other commercially sensitive technical information. (268) Instead, both sets of

         infrastructure providers. The metadata to which Wiz has access does not provide

         capacity planning, or future competitive behaviour, nor does it enable Google to
         influence or predict competitive dynamics in the cloud infrastructure and data
         analytics market. (269) As noted by a CNAPP competitor in the response to the
         Commission’s market investigation: ‘This data reflects configuration state and

(285)       In view of the above considerations and in light of the results of the market

(288)      According to the Non-Horizontal Guidelines, in the majority of circumstances,
           conglomerate mergers will not lead to any competition problems. (308)
(289)      However, foreclosure effects may arise when the combination of products in
           related markets may confer on the merged entity the ability and incentive to
           leverage a strong market position from one market to another closely related
           market by means of tying or bundling or other exclusionary practices. While tying
           and bundling have often no anticompetitive consequences, in certain
           circumstances such practices may lead to a reduction in actual or potential
           competitors’ ability or incentive to compete. This may reduce the competitive
           pressure on the merged entity allowing it to increase prices. (309)
(290)      In assessing the likelihood of such a scenario, the Commission examines, first,
           whether the merged firm would have the ability to foreclose its competitors, (310)
           second, whether it would have the economic incentive to do so (311) and, third,
           whether a foreclosure strategy would have a significant detrimental effect on
           competition, thus causing harm to consumers. (312) These factors are cumulative
           and often examined together as they are closely intertwined.
(291)      In order to be able to foreclose competitors, the merged entity must have a
           significant degree of market power, which does not necessarily amount to
           dominance, in one of the markets concerned. The effects of bundling or tying can
           only be expected to be substantial when at least one of the merging parties’
           products is viewed by many customers as particularly important and there are few
           relevant alternatives for that product. (313) Further, for foreclosure to be a potential
           concern, it must be the case that there is a large common pool of customers, which
           is more likely to be the case when the products are complementary. (314) Finally,
           bundling is less likely to lead to foreclosure if rival firms are able to deploy
           effective and timely counterstrategies, such as single-product companies
           combining their offers. (315)
(292)      The incentive to foreclose competitors through bundling or tying depends on the
           degree to which this strategy is profitable. (316) Bundling and tying may entail
           losses or foregone revenues for the merged entity. (317) However, they may also
           allow the merged entity to increase profits by gaining market power in the tied
           goods market, protecting market power in the tying good market, or a combination
           of the two. (318)
(293)      It is only when a sufficiently large fraction of market output is affected by
           foreclosure resulting from the concentration that the concentration may
           significantly impede effective competition. If there remain effective single-
           product players in either market, competition is unlikely to deteriorate following

5.6.4.1. Technical integration between cloud infrastructure and CNAPP tools
(315)    At a technical level, there are three principal ways in which CNAPPs and cloud
         infrastructures interact: (i) via APIs, (ii) via agents, and (iii) via log exports.
(316)    CNAPPs can use API integrations with cloud infrastructures to take snapshots
         of the cloud infrastructure environment (agentless scanning) and/or connect to
         specific cloud infrastructure components (e.g., compute engine).
(317)    In addition to the agentless, public API-based snapshot approach, CNAPPs can
         also integrate with cloud infrastructures via agents. An agent can be deployed
         immediately on any cloud. Agent-based scanning involves the installation of
         lightweight agents directly on compute resources like virtual machines,
         containers, or Kubernetes nodes to perform specific security or monitoring
         functions. The agent monitors and collects data on the security state of the cloud
         environment and sends data to the CNAPP for processing and visualization of
         alerts, risk scores, and remediation suggestions.
(318)    Logs are formatted text records that capture events and activities as they occur in
         a cloud environment. Each cloud infrastructure provider has its own syntax or log
         format that is specific to that cloud, but the syntax or log format is well-
         documented and made available to allow customers to collect and process the data
         as needed without the need to deploy additional sensors or agents or provide API
         access to their workloads. Customers can configure their cloud infrastructures to
         export different types of logs (i.e., focusing on different types of events). Of these,
         CNAPPs collect security-related logs – such as cloud identity security logs,
         network activity logs, and DNS logs – to enable security visibility, risk detection,
         and compliance monitoring. Log exports are pulled by a CNAPP (with the
         requisite customer permissions). (338)

        5.6.6.   Leveraging of Google’s position in specialised generative AI
(390)      The Notifying Party has identified one potential market where Google’s market
           share exceeds 30% that may be considered as being closely related to the market
           for CNAPP, namely a potential market for specialised generative AI.
(391)      Some CNAPPs integrate third-party generative AI tools to improve threat
           detection and response capabilities or enhance user interfaces (e.g., chatbots)
           without the customer needing to separately license generative AI tools from third
           parties. Others rely on in-house generative AI technologies. Customers can also
           use their preferred generative AI models to process inputs or outputs of CNAPPs
           with or without integration. (407)
(392)      Generative AI is used thus as an input for CNAPPs, and Google has a market
           share of [30-40]% in a potential market for specialised generative AI, as described
           above in Section 5.3.3.
(393)      The Commission has examined whether the merged entity would have the ability
           and incentive, post-Transaction, to foreclose rival providers of CNAPPs by
           leveraging Google’s position in the market for specialised generative AI. In this
           regard, in its market investigation, the Commission has sought to verify whether
           the merged entity would likely have the ability and the incentive to refuse or
           degrade access to Google’s specialised generative AI.

(394)      The Notifying Party submits that there may be some complementarity and
           commonality of customers between generative AI models and CNAPPs, but that
           this relationship does not give rise to competition concerns.

(400)    Contrary to the Notifying Party’s submission, the results of the market

         that specialised generative AI is used in security products to some extent. (415)

         products, this Section assesses whether the merged entity would have the ability
         or incentive to foreclose competing CNAPP suppliers by refusing or degrading
         their access to Google’s specialised generative AI.

5.6.6.1.2.1.    Ability to foreclose
(402)    For the reasons set out below and based on the results of the market investigation,
         the Commission has reached the conclusion that the merged entity would not have
         the ability to engage in a foreclosure strategy vis-à-vis rival CNAPP suppliers by
         refusing or degrading access to Google’s specialised generative AI models.
(403)    First, Google does not have a significant degree of market power in a potential
         worldwide market for specialised generative AI. The market investigation
         confirmed that there are many alternatives to Google in the generative AI space,
         including specialised generative AI. These alternatives include OpenAI, Mistral
         AI and Databricks, (417) as is confirmed by Gartner’s report mentioned in
         Section 5.3.3. (418) Today, Google does not offer specialised generative AI models
         for cybersecurity use cases.
(404)    Second, only 23% of CNAPP competitors consider specialised generative AI to
         be a material input for CNAPP tools. (419) While a large majority of CNAPP
         competitors and approximately half of the responding customers expects
         significant developments in terms of the integration of specialised generative AI
         within CNAPP tools (420), the overall feedback from the market investigation
         shows that a lot of continued innovation is expected, including from new
         companies attracting investments. (421) One respondent explained that ‘[t]he
         cloud computing industry, including cloud security, is highly dynamic, rapidly
         evolving, and characterised by unprecedented levels of investment and
         innovation’ and that ‘[t]he increasing prevalence of AI-driven workloads and
         tools is also disrupting the market and creating opportunities for new and existing
         entrants.’ Another respondent said that they expect to see continued innovation
         and ‘significant VC funding, new companies all the time.’ (422)
(405)    Therefore, the Commission concludes that the merged entity would not have the
         ability to engage in any foreclosure strategy by refusing or degrading access to
         Google’s specialised generative AI models.
