5.1.      Product market definitions

5.1.1.    Compressor trains
(25)      A compressor train typically consists of one or more compressors and a driver,
          together with the associated auxiliary systems. Compressor trains are used to
          compress and circulate refrigerant gases throughout the LNG plant. They increase
          gas pressure to enable cooling in order to liquefy natural gas and to maintain the
          pressure and flow conditions required for efficient and reliable plant operation.
          The driver supplies the mechanical power necessary to operate the compressor
          and may consist of a gas turbine, steam turbine, or electric motor, depending on
          the plant design.

5.1.1.1. Commission’s precedents
(26)      In its past decisional practice, the Commission concluded that the relevant product
          market in oil and gas is the entire compressor train rather than compressors on
          their own. (25)
(27)      The Commission has previously assessed the product market definition for
          compressor trains in the oil and gas industry and considered a distinct product
          market for compressor trains driven by light industrial gas turbines and aero
          derivate gas turbines. (26) The Commission considered a further segmentation by
          power requirement above and below 23 MW but ultimately left the exact
          definition open. (27) However, the Commission’s practice does not refer
          specifically to compressor trains for use in LNG plants.

5.1.1.2. Notifying Party’s views
(28)      The Notifying Party submits that there is no separate market for compressors and
          drivers, which are typically supplied together as part of a compressor trains, but
          rather form part of a broader market for compressor trains. (28)
(29)      Likewise, in the Notifying Party’s view, there is no separate market(s) specifically
          for compressor trains for LNG plants. Instead, it is submitted that compressor
          trains for LNG plants form part of a broader market for compressor trains serving
          a range of applications in the oil and gas sector and other industrial processes. In
          any event, according to the Notifying Party, the market definition can be left open,
          since there is no affected market. (29)
(30)      Nor, in the Notifying Party’s view, is any further segmentation warranted on the
          basis of the size of the compressor trains, the LNG plant’s capacity or the type of
          driver. (30)

(31)    The Commission’s market investigation confirms that compressor trains must be
        considered as a relevant systems market as compressors and drivers are procured
        together. Compressor train suppliers overwhelmingly confirmed that compressors
        and drivers for LNG liquefaction are typically procured together as an integrated
        compressor train package. (31) Likewise, the majority of responding plant owners
        and EPC contractors submit that they (or their clients) usually procure
        compressors and drivers for LNG liquefaction plants together. (32)
(32)    It appears that the procurement of a compressor train consisting of a compressor
        and a driver is often the most efficient approach. (33) In the market investigation,
        plant owners explained that they prefer to procure both components as an
        integrated compressor train as it minimizes the risk of integration between both
        components and they can rely on one other party that takes over the responsibility
        for the train’s operation. (34) Further, plant owners named several other factors
        supporting this finding such as interface design, performance guarantees,
        warranty coverage and risk mitigation. (35)
(33)    Further, the Commission finds that compressor trains to liquefy natural gas cannot
        be substituted with compressor trains used for other applications. The market
        investigation confirms that compressor trains used to liquefy natural gas must be
        specifically designed for the LNG specific use case. (36) This view was supported
        in the market investigation by a large majority of compressor train suppliers for
        LNG liquefaction plants and of EPC contractors. (37) Even if it may be technically
        possible to adapt other compressors trains for LNG liquefaction, it is usually not
        commercially viable due to the high costs associated with the required
        modifications. (38) One compressor train supplier explained that in “most LNG

        components) with ones not specifically designed for LNG service is high-risk and
        often impractical without major modification, re-rating and validation”. (39)

(39)      Process technology refers to the overall design and engineering framework of a
          liquefaction unit, meaning the process through which natural gas is cooled and
          converted into a liquid. It defines the key engineering parameters, the required
          equipment, and the operating conditions of that equipment.

          principles with proprietary knowhow and, in some cases, patented innovations.
          Their expertise lies in optimising process parameters such as refrigerant
          compositions, heat integration strategies, and operating envelopes to maximise
          efficiency, reliability and overall performance of the liquefaction process.

6.2.2.    Legal framework for the competitive assessment of conglomerate effects
(112)     According to the Non-Horizontal Merger Guidelines, in the majority of
          circumstances, conglomerate mergers will not lead to any competition
          problems. (92)
(113)     However, foreclosure effects may arise when the combination of products in
          related markets may confer on the merged entity the ability and incentive to
          leverage a strong market position from one market to another closely related
          market by means of tying or bundling or other exclusionary practices. While tying
          and bundling have often no anticompetitive consequences, in certain
          circumstances such practices may lead to a reduction in actual or potential
          competitors’ ability or incentive to compete. This may reduce the competitive
          pressure on the merged entity allowing it to increase prices. (93)
(114)     In assessing the likelihood of such a scenario, the Commission examines, first,
          whether the merged firm would have the ability to foreclose its rivals, (94) second,
          whether it would have the economic incentive to do so (95) and, third, whether a
          foreclosure strategy would have a significant detrimental effect on competition,
          thus causing harm to consumers. (96) In practice, these factors are often examined
          together as they are closely intertwined.
(115)     In order to be able to foreclose competitors, the merged entity must have a
          significant degree of market power, which does not necessarily amount to
          dominance, in one of the markets concerned. The effects of bundling or tying can
          only be expected to be substantial when at least one of the merging parties’
          products is viewed by many customers as particularly important and there are few
          relevant alternatives for that product. (97) Further, for foreclosure to be a potential
          concern, it must be the case that there is a large common pool of customers, which
          is more likely to be the case when the products are complementary. (98) Finally,
          bundling is less likely to lead to foreclosure if rival firms are able to deploy
          effective and timely counter-strategies, such as single-product companies
          combining their offers. (99)
(116)     The incentive to foreclose rivals through bundling or tying depends on the degree
          to which this strategy is profitable. (100) Bundling and tying may entail losses or
          foregone revenues for the merged entity. (101) However, they may also allow the
          merged entity to increase profits by gaining market power in the tied goods

6.2.4.1.7.      Conclusion on ability
(205)    In light of the above, and in particular of BH’s dominance in the supply of LNG
         compressor trains, the Commission considers that the merged entity would likely
         have the ability to leverage such dominance so as to post-Transaction foreclose
         its rivals in the licensing of process technologies and the supply of MCHE by
         making BH compressor trains available for sale only if purchased together with
         Chart’s LNG process technologies and/or Chart’s LNG MCHE; or by reducing
         the interoperability of BH’s compressor trains with third party equipment (tying);

6.2.4.2. Incentive
(207)    Based on the evidence on the Commission’s file, including the results of the
         market investigation, the Commission considers that BH would likely have the
         incentive to engage in foreclosure strategies with regard to competing providers
         of LNG process technology and LNG MCHE. The incentives for the merged
         entity to engage in these foreclosure strategies depend on the trade-off between
         lost sales in the compressor trains market and the gains it could derive from
         favouring Chart’s process technology and LNG MCHE.
(208)    First, as explained in Sections 6.2.4.1.4 and 6.2.4.1.5, customers and competitors
         alike agreed these strategies are likely.
(209)    Second, the merged entity has multiple different avenues for pursuing a
         foreclosure strategy, and the Parties can offer different commercial conditions on
         the projects on which they bid, such that a foreclosure strategy may focus on
         specific market segments where the strategy would be deemed the most profitable.
         This ability to discriminate reinforces the likelihood of incentives, given that even
         if such foreclosure would turn out not to be profitable on the market as a whole,
         it may well be profitable on certain market segments.
(210)    Third, the merged entity is unlikely to suffer many losses, due to its dominance in
         LNG compressor trains. The merged entity’s market shares in the target markets
         (heat exchangers and process technology) are also comparatively lower, allowing
         for actual gains to be made by the merged entity.

(271)     According to the Non-Horizontal Merger Guidelines, there are two broad types

          two main ways in which a non-horizontal merger may significantly impede

          mergers give rise to foreclosure – i.e., where actual or potential rivals’ access to

          by restricting their access to an important input (input foreclosure), and (ii) where
          the merger is likely to foreclose upstream rivals by restricting their access to a
          sufficient customer base (customer foreclosure). (235) On both types of vertical
          foreclosure, the Commission examines whether the merged entity would have, as
          a result of the merger, the ability to foreclose substantially access to inputs or to

7.1.     Framework for the assessment of the commitments
(292)    Where a concentration raises serious doubts as regards its compatibility with the
         internal market and the functioning of the EEA Agreement, the merging parties
         may undertake to modify the concentration to remove the grounds for the serious
         doubts identified by the Commission. Pursuant to Article 6(2) of the Merger
         Regulation, where the Commission finds that, following modification by the
         undertakings concerned, a notified concentration no longer raises serious doubts,
         it shall declare the concentration compatible with the internal market and with the
         functioning of the EEA Agreement pursuant to Article 6(1)(b) of the Merger
         Regulation.
(293)    As set out in the Commission Notice on remedies acceptable under Council
         Regulation (EC) No 139/2004 and under Commission Regulation (EC) No
         802/2004 (the “Remedies Notice”) (253), the commitments have to eliminate the
         competition concerns entirely, and have to be comprehensive and effective from
         all points of view. (254)
(294)    In assessing whether commitments will maintain effective competition, the
         Commission considers all relevant factors, including the type, scale and scope of

      (a) the Experts shall act fairly and impartially;

      (c) the Experts shall decide the procedure to be followed within five (5) working days
          of their appointment, which may be the rules of arbitration of the London Court of
          International Arbitration;

      (d) the Committing Parties and the Purchaser shall assist and provide documentation
          as the Experts reasonably requires to consider the matters referred to them;

      (e) decisions of the Experts shall be based on majority votes of the panel;

      (f) the Experts’ determination in relation to any matter referred to them shall be given
          within a maximum period of one (1) month of the Experts’ appointment;

      (g) the Experts’ determination in relation to any matter referred to them shall (save

          Purchaser;

      (h) any challenge to the Experts’ determination shall be made in the courts of Milan,
          Italy;
