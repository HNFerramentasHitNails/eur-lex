6.      COMPETITIVE ASSESSMENT

6.1.    Legal framework – non-horizontal mergers
(21)    A merger can entail non-horizontal effects when it involves companies operating
        at different levels of the same supply chain or in closely related markets. Pursuant
        to the Commission Guidelines on the assessment of non-horizontal mergers under
        the Council Regulation on the control of concentrations between undertakings
        (the ‘Non-Horizontal Merger Guidelines’), (31) non-horizontal mergers do not
        entail the loss of direct competition between merging firms in the same relevant
        market and provide scope for efficiencies. However, there are circumstances in
        which non-horizontal mergers may significantly impede effective competition.
        This is in particular the case if they give rise to foreclosure. (32)
(22)    Vertical mergers involve companies operating at different levels of the supply
        chain, for example, when a manufacturer of a certain product merges with one of
        its distributors.
(23)    In assessing potential vertical effects of a merger, the Commission analyses
        whether a merger results in foreclosure so that actual or potential rivals’ access to
        supplies or markets is hampered or eliminated as a result of the merger, thereby
        reducing these companies’ ability and/or incentive to compete. Such foreclosure
        may discourage the entry or expansion of rivals or encourage their exit.
        Foreclosure thus can be found even if the foreclosed rivals are not forced to exit
        the market: it is sufficient that the rivals are disadvantaged and consequently led
        to compete less effectively. As a result of such foreclosure, the merging
        companies – and, possibly, some of their competitors as well – may be able to
        profitably increase the price charged to consumers.
(24)    The Non-Horizontal Merger Guidelines distinguish between two forms of
        foreclosure. (33) The first is where the merger is likely to raise the costs of
        downstream rivals by restricting their access to an important input (input
        foreclosure). The second is where the merger is likely to foreclose upstream rivals
        by restricting their access to a sufficient customer base (customer foreclosure).
(25)    In assessing both types of foreclosure, the Commission applies the ability,
        incentive, effects framework. This implies the assessment of three cumulative
        conditions, i.e. whether (1) the merged entity would have the ability to engage in
        foreclosure, (2) it would have the incentive to do so, and (3) what would be the
        overall impact on effective competition in the affected markets.

6.3.1.    Notifying Party’s view
(29)      According to the Notifying Party, the Transaction does not give rise to a
          significant impediment of effective competition. In terms of input foreclosure, the
          Notifying Party submits that the merged entity would not have the ability or
          incentive to engage in a foreclosure strategy with regards to any of the concerned
          APIs. The main arguments of the Notifying Party are summarised below. (39)
(30)      First, the Notifying Party submits that for each concerned API there are
          alternative suppliers, with the ability to increase production volumes in the event
          Curia would decide to stop or reduce supply. Specifically in relation to
          minocycline HCL, the Notifying Party also notes that it is expected that Chinese
          suppliers will enter the EEA market in the near term.
(31)      Second, the Notifying Party submits that the APIs in question are standard
          commodity products and therefore it is frequent for customers to multi-source and
          switch between API suppliers.
(32)      Third, the Notifying Party submits that Curia would have no incentive to stop
          supplying the APIs in question as (i) due to Zentiva’s limited purchasing
          requirements Curia would lose a significant revenue stream were it to stop
          supplying to third parties, (ii) a foreclosure strategy would put at risk Curia’s
          reputation and customer relationships concerning not only the affected APIs, but
          also the other APIs it sells to the concerned customers, (iii) the proportion of
          volumes that Zentiva could capture downstream following a foreclosure strategy
          is limited, and (iv) given that GTCR only has joint control of Curia (40), it is
          unable to solely determine decisions regarding the operation of the Curia business.
(33)      Finally, the Notifying Party submits that while APIs are not substitutable from
          the FDP manufacturers’ perspective, from the end consumers’ (i.e. the patients’)
          perspective FDPs based on different APIs can be substitutable. (41)
