4.1.2.    Catalent

(18)      As a CDMO, Catalent acts as an outsourcing partner for pharmaceutical
          companies. In this context, it offers, on the one hand, research and development
          services when a product is at the clinical stage of development (referred to as
          “CDO services”) and, on the other hand, product manufacturing services for
          products both at the clinical and commercial stages of their lifecycles (referred to
          as “CMO services”).

(19)      Catalent offers these development and manufacturing services for a wide range of
          molecules and processes, including small molecules and large molecules, whether
          synthetic or biological, and regarding both the active pharmaceutical substance
          (“API”) and the finished drug product (“FDP”).

(20)      With respect to services provided for FDPs, Catalent offers manufacturing services
          referred to as “sterile fill-finish” services, which involves active pharmaceutical
          substance being filled and packaged into containers under sterile conditions. This
          typically includes compounding and mixing of the active pharmaceutical
          substance, filling, closing, sealing, sorting, inspecting, labelling, packaging, and
          quality control checks.

(21)      More specifically, the sterile fill-finish technique involves processing and
          packaging a product into containers, followed by hermetic sealing. The process is
          designed to prevent viable microbial contamination of the drug product. The design
          of the manufacturing environment and equipment in place is an important aspect of
          the fill-finish process. For products administered in the injectable format, such as
          injectable semaglutide, fill-finish involves filling the active pharmaceutical
          substance into one of the following formats: (i) pre-filled syringes (“PFS”), which
          contain a single dose, and are often (but not necessarily) utilized in single-use auto-
          injector pens equipped with a built-in needle; (ii) cartridges, which contain multiple
          doses, and are inserted in durable auto-injector pens equipped with removable

        (a)    The Commission’s past practice

(30)    In its previous decisions, the Commission identified separate markets according to
        whether the product manufactured and supplied was an API or an FDP.25 In Chr.
        Hansen / Lonza / JV, the Commission distinguished the services offered for those
        two types of products. Services at the API level involve the evaluation,
        characterisation, development of production and scaling processes in relation to the
        manufacturing of the actual molecule. Services at FDP level mostly pertain to the
        development of appropriate dosage formulation and large-scale manufacture of the
        final drug product. Additionally, from a supply-side perspective, manufacturing
        these two types of products does not involve the same equipment, know-how and
        expertise, as illustrated by the fact that several CDMO players are exclusively
        active either at the API level or at the FDP level.26

        pharmaceutical form manufactured (e.g. injectables27, semi-solid28, liquids29,

4.2.2.2.1.     CMO FDP services for injectables
         (a)   The Commission’s past practice

(37)     As outlined above, the Commission has previously considered the possibility of
         segmenting the contract manufacturing services at FDP level market based the
         pharmaceutical form manufactured, including solid, semi-solid, injectable, and
         liquid formats43.

(38)     In Novartis / EBEWE, the Commission found that the vast majority of responding
         market participants considered that, with respect to a liquid injectable supplied in
         ampules, vials, and pre-filled syringes (“PFS”), dedicated production facilities and
         know-how were required for each format, but ultimately left open the question
         whether this would justify the delineation of a separate markets based on the format
         of the injectable product.44

(39)     The Commission did not analyse these potential market segmentations further.
         (b)   The Notifying Party’s view

(40)     As mentioned above, the Notifying Party submitted that no further segmentation
         was needed beyond a segmentation between API and FDP services. It however
         acknowledged that there could be an additional segmentation of the injectable
         format, including: (i) PFS; (ii) cartridges; and (iii) vials.45

(41)     With regard to demand-side substitutability between the various formats of
         injectables, the Notifying Party submits46 that each format can be used for a given
         injectable product, and that the different formats are substitutable at each stage of
         the product development, as follows:
               –      In the early stages of product development, particularly during clinical
                      trials, the choice of format is not crucial, and vials are commonly used,
                      notably due to their low marginal per-dose cost.
               –      During later stages of product development, the choice of a format is
                      typically made and feeds into the conditions for marketing
                      authorization applications. Although each format offers different
                      advantages to be assessed by pharmaceutical companies, the different
                      formats are substitutable and serve the same end purpose (delivering a
                      specified dose through an injection). This choice is not influenced by
                      the type of product but comes down to the individual customer’s
                      strategy and preferences. While the PFS format is easily usable for
                      patients leading to reduced medication errors, the vial format may
                      allow for faster scaling47.

5.        COMPETITIVE ASSESSMENT

5.1.      Legal framework

5.1.1.    Horizontal mergers

(102)     A merger can entail horizontal effects. In this respect, the Commission Guidelines
          on the assessment of horizontal mergers under the Council Regulation on the
          control of concentrations between undertakings (the “Horizontal Merger
          Guidelines”) 112 distinguish between two main ways in which mergers between
          actual or potential competitors on the same relevant market may significantly
          impede effective competition, namely (a) by eliminating important competitive
          constraints on one or more firms, which consequently would have increased market
          power, without resorting to coordinated behaviour (non-coordinated effects); and
          (b) by changing the nature of competition in such a way that firms that previously
          were not coordinating their behaviour are now significantly more likely to
          coordinate and raise prices or otherwise harm effective competition. A merger may
          also make coordination easier, more stable or more effective for firms that were
          coordinating prior to the merger (coordinated effects).113

5.1.2.    Non-horizontal mergers

(103)     In addition, a merger can entail non-horizontal effects when it involves companies
          operating at different levels of the same supply chain or in closely related markets.
          Pursuant to the Commission Guidelines on the assessment of non-horizontal
          mergers under the Council Regulation on the control of concentrations between
          undertakings (the “Non-Horizontal Merger Guidelines”),114 non-horizontal
          mergers do not entail the loss of direct competition between merging firms in the
          same relevant market and provide scope for efficiencies. However, there are
          circumstances in which non-horizontal mergers may significantly impede effective
          competition. This is in particular the case if they give rise to foreclosure.115

(104)     Vertical mergers involve companies operating at different levels of the supply
          chain, for example, when a manufacturer of a certain product merges with one of
          its distributors.

(105)     In assessing potential vertical effects of a merger, the Commission analyses
          whether a merger results in foreclosure so that actual or potential rivals’ access to
          supplies or markets is hampered or eliminated as a result of the merger, thereby
          reducing these companies’ ability and/or incentive to compete. Such foreclosure

         companies — and, possibly, some of their competitors as well — are as a result

(106)    The Non-Horizontal Merger Guidelines distinguish between two forms of
         foreclosure.116 The first is where the merger is likely to raise the costs of
         downstream rivals by restricting their access to an important input (input
         foreclosure). The second is where the merger is likely to foreclose upstream rivals
         by restricting their access to a sufficient customer base (customer foreclosure).

(107)    In assessing both types of foreclosure, the Commission applies the ability,
         incentive, effects framework. This implies the assessment of three cumulative
         conditions, i.e. whether (1) the merged entity would have the ability to engage in
         foreclosure, (2) it would have the incentive to do so, and (3) what would be the
         overall impact on effective competition in the affected markets.

         gain access to commercially sensitive information regarding the upstream or
         downstream activities of rivals. For instance, by becoming the supplier of a

         it to price less aggressively in the downstream market to the detriment of
         consumers. Such acquisition of commercially sensitive information may also put
         competitors at a competitive disadvantage, thereby dissuading them from entering
         or expanding in the market.117
