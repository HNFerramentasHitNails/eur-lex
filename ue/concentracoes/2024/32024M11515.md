4.2.1.    Product market definition

4.2.1.1. The Commission’s decisional practice

(20)      The Commission has previously taken the view that the provision of balancing and
          ancillary services is distinct from the market for the generation and wholesale
          supply of electricity.33

(21)      The Commission has previously considered whether balancing and ancillary
          services should be divided into markets at least for (i) FCR, (ii) aFRR, and
          (iii) mFRR, based on the order of activation and the magnitude of frequency
          deviation.34 While in the past leaving this question open,35 the Commission has in a
          recent decision, considered that there are separate product markets for the provision
          of FCR, aFRR and mFRR services.36

(22)      Within aFRR, the Commission has considered segmenting between the upward
          (aFRR+) and downward (aFRR-) regulation of aFRR, but ultimately left the
          question open.37

4.2.1.2. The Notifying Party’s view

(23)      The Notifying Party considers that the relevant product market is the market for
          balancing and ancillary services without any further segmentation. The Notifying
          Party however submits that the market definition can ultimately be left open as no
          competition concerns would arise from the Transaction.38

(24)      First, aFRR and mFRR are substitutable to a significant extent: aFRR-accredited
          power plants can and do compete for the provision of mFRR services in Hungary.
          Moreover, players in the mFRR segment can shift to aFRR with limited
          investment.39

(25)      Second, from a demand perspective, FCR, aFRR, and mFRR can be used to meet
          the same need, with the activation time being the main difference. In Hungary,
          small imbalance levels are covered with FCR, which can be automatically activated
          and fully ramped up within seconds. However, FCR is generally limited in
          Hungary due to the limited sources of electricity generation suitable for immediate

(28)    All respondents to the Commission’s market investigation that expressed a view
        confirmed the Commission’s past practice that the provision of balancing and
        ancillary services is a distinct market, separate from the generation and wholesale
        supply of electricity.43 The Hungarian NCA (GVH) has adopted a similar approach
        in Hungary, distinguishing balancing and ancillary services from the electricity
        generation and wholesale market in its decisional practice.44

(29)    With regard to the distinction between FCR, aFRR and mFRR, the market
        investigation results suggest that FCR constitutes a separate market from aFRR and
        mFRR, given that not all generation units active in the provision of aFRR or mFRR
        services are also active in FCR.45 Regarding the possible distinction between aFRR
        and mFRR, Mavir notes that “the accreditation process is different depending on
        the type of service” and that the requirements to prequalify for mFRR are less
        stringent than for aFRR, resulting in aFRR-accredited plants being technically
        suitable to provide mFRR services but not the opposite.46 As a result, the
        generation technologies used for mFRR are much more varied than for aFRR
        services: while predominantly natural gas-fired plants and photovoltaic plants were
        pre-qualified to provide aFRR services in 2023, mFRR services were provided, in
        addition to natural gas and photovoltaics, from a mix of generation sources based
        on biogas, biomass, diesel, lignite, nuclear and oil. Indeed, around 40% of the
        mFRR-accredited capacity is not accredited for aFRR.47 Based on these elements,
        the Commission considers that there are separate markets for (i) FCR, (ii) aFRR
        and (iii) mFRR.

(43)      Horizontal effects are those deriving from a concentration where the undertakings
          concerned are actual or potential competitors of each other in one or more of the
          relevant markets concerned. The Horizontal Merger Guidelines distinguish
          between two main ways in which mergers between actual or potential competitors
          on the same relevant market may significantly impede effective competition,
          namely non-coordinated and coordinated effects.70 This Decision focuses on non-
          coordinated effects.

(44)      Non-coordinated effects may significantly impede effective competition through
          the creation or strengthening of the dominant position of a single firm, or through
          the elimination of important competitive constraints on one or more firms, which
          consequently would allow those firms to have increased market power without
          resorting to coordinated behaviour. In that regard, the Horizontal Merger
          Guidelines consider not only the direct loss of competition between the merging
          firms, but also the reduction in competitive pressure on non-merging firms in the
          same market that could be brought about by the merger.71

(45)      The Horizontal Merger Guidelines list a number of factors to be taken into
          consideration when assessing whether significant non-coordinated effects are likely
          to result from a merger, such as large market shares of the merging firms, the fact
          that the merging firms are close competitors, the limited possibilities for customers
          to switch suppliers, or the fact that the merger would eliminate an important
          competitive force. Not all of these factors need to be present to make non-
          coordinated effects likely, nor does this constitute an exhaustive list.72

(46)      According to the Horizontal Merger Guidelines, in assessing the competitive
          effects of a concentration, the Commission compares the competitive conditions
          that would result from the notified concentration with the conditions that would
          have prevailed without the concentration. Whereas this comparison usually
          compares the likely effects of a concentration with the competitive conditions
          existing at the time of the merger, the Commission may, in some circumstances,
          take into account future changes to the market that can reasonably be predicted.73
          Such reasonably predictable future changes may arise in particular, but not only,
          where firms are likely to enter or exit the market absent the concentration.74

(128)    Whereas market shares provide useful first indications of the market structure and

         competitors,236 the Commission notes at the outset that in the market for the
         provision of aFRR energy, where BSPs offer aFRR energy on a quarter-hourly
         basis, market shares are of limited informative value, especially since they are
         based on an annual average. The Commission has nevertheless taken the Parties’
         market share estimates into account for its overall assessment of the Transaction.

(129)    As shown above, the Parties’ combined market share in the aFRR energy market
         overall amounted to [60-70]% in 2023, with an increment of [20-30] percentage
         points. On a narrower basis, the combined market share was as high as [60-70]% in
         the segments for aFRR- energy in the winter season of 2023, with an increment of
         [20-30] percentage points. Pursuant to established case-law, very large market
         shares, i.e. 50% or more, may in themselves be evidence of the existence of a
         dominant market position.237

(130)    Indeed, with a high combined market share in aFRR energy activation, the Parties
         have a strong market position due to their efficiency and consequent low prices and
         high ramp up rates. The Hungarian TSO activates bids based on price and the
         capacity available within 1 minute. Due to their superior efficiency and higher
         ramp-up rates, the Parties are able to deliver more MW of energy within 1 minute
         and at a lower price than their rivals. For this reason, and as presented in section
         5.2.1.4, pre-merger, the Parties pose a significant competitive constraint on each
         other, offering relatively lower prices and higher ramp-up rates, while competitors’
         prices are relatively higher. All else being equal, the transaction therefore has the
         potential to increase prices, by eliminating the competitive constraint that the
         Parties impose on each other.

5.2.2.4.1.      Hungary’s accession to the PICASSO platform will result in increased

(133)    The information gathered during the market investigation from market participants,
         the Parties, the Hungarian TSO (Mavir) and other European TSOs, as well as
         ACER239 and ENTSO-E240 supports the Notifying Party’s argument that Hungary’s
         anticipated accession to PICASSO will lead to aFRR energy market integration
         among participating countries and increased competitive pressure for current BSPs
         in Hungary by BSPs from all participating countries.

(134)    PICASSO allows participating TSOs to pool their aFRR energy resources and to
         activate aFRR energy from the cheapest available BSPs across all participating
         countries.241 BSPs from countries participating in PICASSO submit their aFRR
         energy offers to their usual TSO. These offers are then forwarded to PICASSO, and
         are combined into a Common Merit Order List (‘CMOL’). The CMOL is based on
         the local merit order lists of all participating countries’ TSOs. TSOs submit their
         aFRR energy activation requests to PICASSO, on the basis of which an algorithm
         (so-called Activation Optimization Function or ‘AOF’) determines the most
         efficient activation, based on the CMOL (i.e., cheaper offers are prioritized)242 and
         other factors, such as network congestion and available interconnector capacity.243
         The AOF matches TSOs’ activation requests with the cheapest available energy
         bids and TSOs cannot directly choose which aFRR energy bids are activated to
         address their needs.244 Conversely, BSPs have no insight into the extent to which
         their aFRR energy will be activated for cross-border supply.245 Moreover, the
         AOF’s optimization process does not include priority access to local volumes.246
