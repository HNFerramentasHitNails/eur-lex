3.1.      Waterproofing membranes

3.1.1.    Product market definition

(13)      Waterproofing encompasses an array of different products used to protect concrete
          structures against water, including waterproofing membranes of the kind produced
          by the Parties.

(14)      Both Parties produce waterproofing membranes, which are primarily used to create
          watertight coverings on roofs. They are generally manufactured from either
          bitumen (i.e. asphalt) or synthetic (plastic) membranes.

(15)      Bitumen waterproofing membranes are made by combining asphalt with felts, mats
          or fabrics of organic or inorganic fibres. Producers generally modify the bitumen
          by adding other materials during the production process (to ease installation and
          enhance performance). These modifications include the following two main
          product categories of bitumen waterproofing membranes: (i) atactic polypropylene
          modified bitumen waterproofing membranes (‘APP’, a plastomer, i.e.
          thermoplastic); and (ii) styrene-butadiene-styrene modified bitumen waterproofing
          membranes (‘SBS’, i.e. rubber).

(16)      Synthetic waterproofing membranes are made of a variety of polymeric compounds
          and do not require a carrier layer such as felts, mats or other fabrics.

3.2.      Underlays

3.2.1.    Product market definition

(35)      Underlays are used in both roofing and vertical applications (walls and façades).
          While waterproofing membranes are applied on the outside of a construction,
          underlays are placed as a further waterproofing layer below a building’s surface.
          Underlays are typically also made of bitumen or synthetic materials.

(36)      Underlays may be distinguished according to vapour permeability: while breather
          underlays are vapour- permeable, non-breather underlays do not allow water
          vapour to pass from inside to outside a building. This means that non-breather
          underlays are used in combination with a ventilation system. Conversely, building
          structures without a ventilation system require a breather underlay so the moisture
          is not caught inside the building.

3.2.1.1. Notifying Party’s views

(37)      The Notifying Party submits that breathability, material, and application of the
          underlay represent parameters differentiating distinct product offerings.37
          Breathable underlays are made of synthetic materials, while non-breathable
          underlays are almost exclusively made of bitumen.38 As for application, the
          Notifying Party submits that it has limited visibility with respect to the end use of
          the underlays, but notes that underlays are typically used in connection with
          pitched roofs, as well as in vertical applications.39

(38)      On this basis, the Notifying Party considers that the market can be segmented
          between bitumen/non-breather underlays and synthetic/breather underlays, which
          would be the narrowest plausible markets.40

3.2.1.2. The Commission’s assessment

(39)      The Commission has in one precedent assessed whether pitched roof underlays
          should be considered as a separate product market, and further segmented between
          bitumen and plastic.41 In another precedent, the Commission considered the market
          for breathable membranes (as opposed to non-breathable membranes) for building
          applications, and whether these should be segmented between façade and roof
          applications.42 In both precedents, the Commission ultimately left the product
          market definition open.

4.      COMPETITIVE ASSESSMENT

4.1.    Legal framework

(49)    Article 2 of the Merger Regulation requires the Commission to examine whether
        notified concentrations are compatible with the internal market, by assessing
        whether they would significantly impede effective competition in the internal
        market or in a substantial part of it.52 The Commission’s Guidelines on the
        assessment of horizontal mergers under the Merger Regulation (the ‘Horizontal
        Merger Guidelines’) distinguish two main ways in which mergers between actual
        or potential competitors on the same relevant market may significantly impede
        effective competition, namely non-coordinated effects and coordinated effects.53

(50)    Non-coordinated effects may significantly impede effective competition by
        eliminating the competitive constraint imposed by one party to the concentration on
        the other, as a result of which the Combined Entity would have increased market
        power without resorting to coordinated behaviour. According to recital 25 of the
        Merger Regulation, a significant impediment to effective competition can result
        from the anticompetitive effects of a concentration even if the Combined Entity
        would not have a dominant position on the market concerned. In this regard, the
        Horizontal Merger Guidelines consider not only the direct loss of competition
        between the merging firms, but also the reduction in competitive pressure on non-
        merging firms in the same market that could be brought about by the merger.54
