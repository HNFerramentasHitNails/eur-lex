3.1.1.    Product market definition

3.1.1.1. Commission precedents

(11)      In previous decisions, the Commission has considered several potential
          segmentations of the market for the supply of semiconductors (which encompasses
          GPUs) including based on the processor type, the semiconductor type, and the end
          use. The Commission found that discrete GPUs belong to a separate market than
          CPUs, integrated GPUs, field-programmable gate arrays (‘FPGAs’) and
          Application Specific Integrated Circuits (‘ASICs’).9 Specifically for use in
          datacentres, the Commission concluded that:
          (a)    A distinct product market existed for discrete GPUs, separate from integrated
                 GPUs, as the latter mainly support light acceleration tasks, while discrete
                 GPUs have become the default solution for specific parallel workloads in
                 datacentres.10
          (b)    For demanding parallelised workloads, other types of accelerators such as
                 FPGAs and ASICs are also not suitable alternatives to discrete GPUs. This is
                 because different types of data processing solutions are suitable for different
                 kinds of tasks. ASICs were found to be typically designed for very specific
                 workloads and particular customers, lacking flexibility and a built-in software
                 environment, and were therefore not a suitable option for customers with
                 limited resources who need to perform a range of different HPC workloads.
                 Similarly, FPGAs were found to be unsuitable for most HPC applications
                 because, compared to GPUs, they were relatively inefficient in terms of
                 energy consumption, cost more and were more difficult to program.11

3.1.1.3. Commission’s assessment

(18)    In the market investigation, the Commission tested whether, for use in datacentres,
        there is a separate market for discrete GPUs or whether such a market should
        include (i) integrated GPUs; (ii) other types of accelerators such as ASICs,
        application-specific standard products (‘ASSPs’), FGPAs, or other accelerators.
        Furthermore, in view of the Notifying Party’s arguments, the Commission tested
        whether the market should include renting accelerated compute capacity as a
        service from cloud-based solutions.

(19)    In relation to integrated GPUs, the results of the market investigation indicated that
        for the use in datacentres, integrated GPUs do not form part of the same market as
        discrete GPUs. From the demand-side perspective, the majority of customers
        expressing a view in the market investigation consider that integrated GPUs are not
        a suitable alternative to discrete GPUs for use in datacentres.20 A number of
        respondents explain that this is due to an important difference in performance and
        scalability between integrated and discrete GPUs, making integrated GPUs less
        suitable for running high performance workloads in datacentres.21

(20)    From the supply-side perspective, most of the respondents to the market
        investigation expressing a view consider that a supplier of integrated GPUs would
        not be able to start offering discrete GPUs for use in datacentres in a timely manner
        and without incurring significant costs.22 Market participants indicate that this
        results from the different requirements of both products, which translates into
        different design needs. Furthermore, some market participants indicate that
        suppliers of integrated GPUs wanting to switch to the supply of discrete GPUs
        would need to also develop an associated software stack, which would take a
        significant amount of time and investment.23

(21)    In relation to other accelerators, the results of the market investigation indicated
        that for use in datacentres, other accelerators such as ASICs, ASSPs, FPGAs or
        other accelerators do not form part of the same market as discrete GPUs. From a
        demand-side perspective, the majority of customers expressing a view in the
        market investigation do not consider other accelerators to be a suitable alternative
        to discrete GPUs for use in datacentres.24 A number of respondents explain that
        other accelerators are specialised processors programmed to perform a specific
        task, and do not have the same general-purpose nature, flexibility, and
        computational performance of a GPU. They would therefore be suitable
        alternatives for very specific workloads at most, or indeed be complements rather
        than supplements.25

3.1.2.    Geographic market definition

3.1.2.1. Commission precedents

(31)      In previous decisions, the Commission considered that the potential market for
          discrete datacentre GPUs is worldwide in scope, regardless of the exact product
          market definition, as products are supplied on a worldwide basis and conditions of
          competition do not differ depending on the location of the datacentre of the end-
          customer.40

3.1.2.2. The Notifying Party’s view

(32)      The Notifying Party submits that the geographic scope of the market is worldwide
          given the global production and distribution of datacentre accelerators, low
          transportation costs and global competition.41

3.1.2.3. Commission’s assessment

(33)      In the market investigation, the Commission investigated the geographic scope of
          the market for GPUs for use in datacentres, or any alternative market considered in
          Section 3.1.1. The results of the market investigation indicated that the scope of the
          market is worldwide. Almost all respondents to the market investigation indicated
          that competition takes place on a global level,42 as customers source at a global
          level, competitors are active across the globe, the products do not vary by
          geography and shipping costs are minimal.43

(34)      In light of the above and in line with its previous decisional practice, for the
          purposes of this decision, the Commission concludes that the geographic scope of
          the market for the supply of discrete GPUs for use in datacentres is worldwide.

3.2.    Market for the supply of GPU orchestration software

(35)    Run:ai offers GPU orchestration software, which allows Run:ai’s customers44 to
        schedule, manage and optimise their compute infrastructure, including GPUs, for
        AI workloads, whether on-premises, in the cloud or in hybrid environments.45
        Run:ai’s GPU orchestration software “orchestrates” the allocations of shared
        datacentre GPUs among different AI workloads or jobs, so that multiple teams or
        users can use the same datacentre GPU clusters46 at different times. The workload
        would be an AI service that, for example, is a chat bot, a speech recognition or
        service for transcription.47 The GPUs are virtualised into a pool that can be
        allocated dynamically to the different AI workloads (belonging to business units or
        individuals) based on guaranteed quotas rather than just namespaces. This allows
        Run:ai’s customers to get access to more computing power, run more AI
        workloads, and be more productive.

(37)    Run:ai’s GPU orchestration software has several features, including:
        (a)    Single pane of glass, also known as analytics or workload visibility: the
               offering of a unified, centralised dashboard or interface that provides
               comprehensive visibility and management of all GPU resources, scheduling
               activities, and workload status across the infrastructure.
        (b)    GPU fractioning, also known as vGPUs or GPU sharing: the division of
               GPUs into several “logical GPUs” so that customers can run multiple
               workloads in containers51 side-by-side on the same GPU without interfering
               with each other.
        (c)    GPU dynamic scheduling: ability to adaptively allocate and reallocate GPU
               resources in real-time based on the changing demands and priorities of
               different tasks or workloads.
        (d)    GPU node pooling: ability to group (or “pool”) a set of nodes (i.e., the
               physical machines that can be used to run workloads) based on a common
               feature or property, such as GPU type. The software can then direct specific
               types of workloads to the best-suited node pool for that workload type. For
               example, an organisation may want to direct certain workloads to its highest-
               end GPUs.
        (e)    Security auditability, also known as compliance: role-based access control to

4.1.    Introduction

(82)    Under Article 2(2) and (3) of the Merger Regulation, the Commission must assess
        whether a proposed concentration would significantly impede effective competition
        in the internal market or in a substantial part of it, in particular through the creation
        or strengthening of a dominant position.

(83)    In this respect, a merger may entail horizontal and/or non-horizontal (namely,
        vertical or conglomerate) effects. Horizontal effects are those deriving from a
        concentration where the undertakings concerned are actual or potential competitors

4.3.1.    Legal framework

(101)     According to the Non-Horizontal Guidelines, in the majority of circumstances,
          conglomerate mergers will not lead to any competition problems.105

(102)     Conglomerate effects may principally arise when non-horizontal mergers give rise
          to foreclosure. Foreclosure effects may arise when the combination of products in
          related markets may confer on the merged entity the ability and incentive to
          leverage a strong market position from one market to another closely related
          market by means of tying or bundling or other exclusionary practices. While tying
          and bundling have often no anticompetitive consequences, in certain circumstances
          such practices may lead to a reduction in actual or potential competitors’ ability or
          incentive to compete. This may reduce the competitive pressure on the merged
          entity allowing it to increase prices.106

(103)     In assessing the likelihood of such a scenario, the Commission examines, first,
          whether the merged firm would have the ability to foreclose its competitors107,
          second, whether it would have the economic incentive to do so108 and, third,
          whether a foreclosure strategy would have a significant detrimental effect on
          competition, thus causing harm to consumers.109 In practice, these factors are often
          examined together as they are closely intertwined.

          significant degree of market power, which does not necessarily amount to
          dominance, in one of the markets concerned. The effects of bundling or tying can

         comes with higher personnel costs’.207 Specifically in relation to using alternative
         frameworks, one respondent explains that ‘Switching to a different vendor, like
         AMD or Intel, often requires reconfiguring or rewriting code to use alternative
         frameworks such as ROCm or OneAPI, which is costly and time-consuming’.208

(158)    A further lack of buyer power is demonstrated by a majority indicating that they
         would not have the bargaining power to react to a price increase of the Parties’
         products.209 This ties into the lock-in that customers experience. Respondents to the
         market investigation explain that: ‘Many end-users are locked into specific
         ecosystems, such as Nvidia’s CUDA, which makes switching to alternatives costly
         and time-consuming. This reduces their ability to react to price increases’ and that
         ‘high switching costs (e.g., retraining staff, rewriting software) often make
         immediate reactions difficult’.210 Therefore, the Commission concludes that
         customers do not exert significant bargaining power vis-à-vis NVIDIA in the
         market for discrete GPUs for the use in datacentres. While some respondents
         indicate that there may be a difference in bargaining power depending on the size
         of the customer (hyperscalers vs. small customers), the Commission considers that
         even hyperscalers will have a continued need for discrete GPUs and could not
         replace their entire demand to proprietary solutions, as described in more detail
         above at recital 152. Furthermore, any room for negotiation would still be
         constrained by high switching costs and current supply chain issues.

4.3.3.2.2.      Conclusion

(228)    In view of the above considerations and in light of the results of the market
         investigation and the evidence and information available to it, the Commission
         concludes that the Transaction does not raise serious doubts as to its compatibility
         with the internal market as a result of conglomerate effects, notably by foreclosing
         competing suppliers of discrete GPUs for use in datacentres, considering that the
         merged entity would not have the ability to engage in such strategy. The
         Commission thus considers that the Transaction will not have a negative impact on
         customers of discrete GPUs for use in datacentre in the EEA, including in Italy.270
