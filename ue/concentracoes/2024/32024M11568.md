Guarantee mechanism

(46)     The Italian regulatory framework48 gives LNG terminals designated as strategic

         revenues are lower than allowed revenues.

(47)     In practice, if the actual revenues achieved within a calendar year by a certain LNG
         terminal are less than 64% of the allowed revenues, this terminal receives the
         difference between its actual revenue and 64% of its allowed revenue50 – the
         guarantee mechanism thereby ensures that each LNG terminal reaches 64% of its
         allowed revenues in a given year. This difference is paid to the terminal by the
         Energy and Environmental Services Fund ‘Cassa per i servizi energetici
         ambientali’ (‘CSEA’)51.

(48)     If the actual revenues achieved by a LNG terminal are between 64% and 100% of
         its allowed revenues, this terminal is not entitled to a contribution by CSEA.

(49)     If a LNG terminal achieves within a calendar year actual revenues that are above
         its allowed revenues, this LNG terminal is required to rebate 64% of the revenues
         in excess of the allowed revenues in the form of a reduction of future regasification
         tariffs.

(50)     LNG terminals under the guarantee mechanism cannot opt out52.

5.2.      Non-horizontal effects

5.2.1.    Analytical framework

(121)     The legal test for the assessment of non-horizontal effects of a merger is set out in
          the Merger Regulation and the Commission’s Guidelines on the assessment of non-
          horizontal mergers (‘Non-horizontal Merger Guidelines’).145.

(122)     Non-horizontal effects can be distinguished between vertical and conglomerate
          effects146.

(123)     According to the Non-horizontal Merger Guidelines, foreclosure effects in a
          vertical scenario may occur where actual or potential rivals’ access to supplies (i.e.
          input foreclosure) or markets (i.e. customer foreclosure) is hampered or eliminated
          as a result of the merger, thereby reducing these companies’ ability and/or
          incentive to compete147.

(124)     In assessing the likelihood of an anticompetitive input foreclosure scenario, the
          Commission examines whether the merged entity would have, post-merger, the
          ability to substantially foreclose access to inputs, whether it would have the
          incentive to do so, and whether a foreclosure strategy would have a significant
          detrimental effect on competition downstream148. Those conditions are
          cumulative149.

(125)     Similarly, in assessing the likelihood of an anticompetitive customer foreclosure
          scenario, the Commission examines whether the merged entity would have the
          ability to foreclose access to downstream markets by reducing its purchases from
          its upstream rivals, whether it would have the incentive to reduce its purchases
          upstream, and whether a foreclosure strategy would have a significant detrimental
          effect on consumers in the downstream market150. Those conditions are
          cumulative151.

(126)     Foreclosure effects may also occur in a conglomerate scenario, where the
          combination of products in related markets may confer on the merged entity the

5.2.2.    The non-horizontal relationship between the operation of LNG regasification
          terminals (or gas import infrastructure) and the operation of the high-pressure gas
          transmission network in Italy

(128)     Gas import infrastructures need to be interconnected to the national gas
          transmission system, which requires the construction of an interconnection point in
          the gas transmission network, which is carried out by the operator of the gas
          transmission system. Subsequently, operators of gas import infrastructures require
          access to the transmission system on a rolling basis. Therefore, the Transaction
          will, from the perspective of operators of gas import infrastructure and the operator
          of gas transmission networks, bring about a vertical relationship in Italy between
          the upstream market for infrastructure for gas imports (and its possible sub-
          segment for LNG regasification terminals), where Snam and ALNG are both
          active, and the downstream market for the operation of the gas transmission
          system, where Snam is active as the Italian TSO.

(129)     Furthermore, customers of gas import infrastructure (i.e. gas shippers), once their
          gas loads have reached the Italian transmission network, need transmission services
          to deliver the commodity to their customers. Since customers sequentially buy the
          services of gas import infrastructures and the services of gas transmission
          networks, those are considered neighbouring markets. Therefore, the Transaction
          will, from the perspective of gas shippers, bring about a conglomerate relationship
          in Italy between the market for infrastructure for gas imports, where Snam and
          ALNG are both active, and the market for gas transmission in Italy, where Snam
          has a monopoly.

5.2.3.3.1.        Customer foreclosure

(170)    The Commission does not consider it likely that the Transaction will afford ALNG
         the ability or incentive to foreclose its rivals in the market for gas import
         infrastructure from a sufficient base of customers.

        Ability

(171)    As per the Commission’s Non-Horizontal Merger Guidelines, ‘[f]or customer
         foreclosure to be a concern, it must be the case that the vertical merger involves a
         company which is an important customer with a significant degree of market power
         in the downstream market. If, on the contrary, there is a sufficiently large customer
         base, at present or in the future, that is likely to turn to independent suppliers, the
         Commission is unlikely to raise competition concerns on that ground191.’

(172)    As shown in Table 6 above, Vitol is not an important customer of gas import
         infrastructure and does not hold a significant degree of market power in the market
         for the downstream wholesale of gas in Italy. In 2022, Vitol accounted for only
         2.6% of actual gas imports into Italy192 and for 2.1% of gas supplies in the
         downstream wholesale market. It follows that, even if Vitol would post-
         Transaction switch its entire demand for gas import infrastructure to ALNG, the
         remaining gas import infrastructures in Italy would still have access to a sufficient
         customer base, accounting for more than 97% of demand.

(173)    Moreover, it is not the case that Vitol would post-Transaction be able to simply
         switch its demand for gas import from other infrastructure to ALNG. As explained
         in paragraph (40) above, allocation of capacity at LNG terminals in Italy is strictly
         regulated, and Vitol would not receive preferential access to regasification capacity
         as one of the co-owners of ALNG. Instead, ALNG would have to secure
         regasification capacity at ALNG through competitive auctions, on equal terms with
         its rivals.

(174)    In addition, it is not obvious that Vitol could cover through ALNG its demand that
         is currently served by other gas import infrastructure. As explained in
         paragraph (87) et seq., each gas import infrastructure offers at each auction
         relatively different capacity products in terms of timeframes and volumes. As
         regards ALNG in particular, Vitol could not switch to it for any regasification
         capacity until 2029, and only for very limited volumes until 2034193, due to the
         limited unallocated capacity at ALNG. It follows that Vitol would post-Transaction
         still be reliant on third-party gas import infrastructure to cover its own import
         needs.

        Incentive

(175)    Vitol’s indirect stake in ALNG (through its joint control in VTTI, which, in turn
         will jointly control ALNG) will likely dilute Vitol’s incentive to engage in a
