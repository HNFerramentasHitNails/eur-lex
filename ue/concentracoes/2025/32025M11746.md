(9)       Contract logistics entails the management of the flow of goods and value added-

(15)     The Notifying Party submits that a segmentation by type of transported goods is

4.1.1.2.2.2.     Notifying Party’s views

(29)     The Notifying Party considers that there are no standard definitions of FTL, LTL
         and groupage and that these terms may be used differently across the industry.
         Based on their knowledge of the industry, the Parties consider that the majority of
         their competitors use them in the following way:

               a. Both FTL and LTL refer to direct transport between two locations, without
                  (de-)consolidation via cross-docks. FTL shipments are collected from one
                  shipper and transported directly to one recipient / consignee. LTL
                  shipments are collected from various shippers and delivered directly to
                  various recipients / consignees.

               b. Groupage shipments, on the other hand, are not transported directly from
                  shippers to recipients / consignees, but go through consolidation and de-
                  consolidation at cross-docks. Hence, groupage transportation requires
                  access to an infrastructure / network of cross-docks.26

               c. Groupage is used where the cargo to be transported does not fill a whole

                  shipment is more efficient than an LTL shipment.27

(31)     With respect to a potential segmentation between FTL, LTL, and groupage, the
         Notifying Party submits that demand-side substitutability between the three
         categories is high. Whether a shipment that is not a full truckload is transported as
         an LTL or groupage shipment is generally determined by the freight forwarder on
         the basis of what is more efficient – in light of the cargo’s volume / dimensions, the
         freight forwarder’s particular set-up as well as, e.g., the price offered to the
         customer and the lead time commitments. For the customer, it is typically not
         important, and the customer will not necessarily be informed, whether a shipment
         is LTL or groupage.28 Regarding supply-side substitutability, the Notifying Party
         submits that freight forwarders that are active in FTL can start providing LTL and
         groupage services without building any additional assets and without making
         material investments within a short period of time (less than three to six months).
         The only added element of complexity compared to FTL / LTL for a freight
         forwarder to be able to offer groupage services is the need to consolidate different
         transported goods in a cross-dock (infrastructure). While there is no infrastructure
         required to perform FTL and LTL services, a cross-dock for groupage can easily be
         built, acquired or leased (asset-light business model), as cross-docks are freely
         available on the market for rent from a number of dedicated industrial real estate
         and infrastructure developers.29

4.1.1.2.2.3.     Commission’s assessment

(32)     The market investigation provided mixed results regarding a potential segmentation
         between FTL, LTL, and groupage.

(33)     First, the market investigation was inconclusive regarding demand-side
         substitutability. A majority of competitors and customers that expressed a view
         indicated that, from a customer’s perspective, FTL, LTL, and groupage services are
         substitutable.30 Nevertheless, a majority of competitors and customers that
         expressed a view indicated that customers generally distinguish between FTL,
         LTL, and groupage in their requests for proposals – indicating that customers
         regard that distinction as important and are not indifferent as to which types of
         services they procure.31 A customer explained that ‘We know how many pallets we
         need to load from each supplier so we give exact directions to each forwarder’.32
         Another customer pointed out that ‘Depending on customer demand, order
         frequency and minimum ordering quantities we determine the best and most
         suitable mode of transport for the volumes and split it between FTL, LTL or
         Groupage requirements’.33

4.2.      Contract logistics

4.2.1.    Product market definition

4.2.1.1.1.1.    Commission’s decisional practice

(79)      In previous decisions, the Commission distinguished freight forwarding services
          from contract logistics and has considered that the provision of contract logistics
          services is the part of the supply chain process offered at a warehouse that plans,
          implements, and controls the efficient and effective flow and storage of goods,
          services, and related information from the point of origin to the point of
          consumption in order to meet customers’ requirements. Contract logistics is thus
          understood as the management of the flow of goods at the warehouse, which is
          separate from the transportation or flow of goods itself from and to the warehouse.
          The customer can be the supplier (e.g., factory) or the buyer of the goods (e.g.,
          retailer), but the service is essentially the same in both scenarios and concentrates
          on the static service offered at the warehouse.88 It can include inbound logistics,
          warehouse management (i.e. stock management and order processing), value added
          services (such as assembly, quality control, re-packaging, labelling, returns
          processing, or repairs) and outbound freight management.

(140)    The market investigation suggests that in general, DSV and Schenker are close

         than with several other competitors, overall, or in any sub-segments of the land
         freight forwarding market.149

(141)    Overall, DSV and Schenker can be considered close competitors on the land freight
         forwarding markets in the EEA, since they both have a pan-European presence and
         are active globally, both with well-developed networks of cross-docks. They both
         offer a range of freight forwarding services (i.e. FTL, LTL, and groupage) across
         various industries. They are also both present on the neighbouring markets of air

5.4.1.3. Barriers to entry and expansion

(143)    The Commission’s file shows that there are some barriers to entry, expansion, or
         capacity constraints affecting land freight forwarding. However, the extent of these
         barriers varies across market segments. Offering groupage might be more complex
         and potentially more capital intense than LTL, and LTL more complex than FTL.

(144)    First, small-scale entry into land freight forwarding is not particularly capital
         intensive, as market entry does not require investment in fleets of vehicles or other
         infrastructure. Since freight forwarders are intermediaries between carriers and
         shippers, establishing a network of sub-contractors operating or providing such
         assets, coordinated by way of an IT solution might be sufficient to enter the market.
         At the same time, having a dedicated infrastructure network of cross-docks,
         specialised knowledge and established relationships with multiple transportation
         companies could be relevant obstacles for potential entrants.

5.7.      Contract logistics

5.7.1.    General market dynamics

(389)     Certain factors relevant to the competitive assessment475 of contract logistics
          markets are common to the assessment of all national markets under review, that is
          Denmark and Finland. These are assessed together in sections 5.7.1.1.- 5.7.1.4 .
          below and concern (i) market fragmentation and the limited relevance of scale, (ii)
          closeness of competition between competitors, (iii) barriers to entry and expansion,
          and (iv) the ease of switching suppliers and multi-sourcing. Sections 5.7.2 and
          5.7.3, in turn, include a competitive assessment on a country-by-country-basis.

5.7.1.1. Market fragmentation and the limited relevance of scale

(390)     Similarly to freight forwarding markets, contract logistics markets appear to be
          fragmented with competitors of different sizes, including larger pan-European
          players such as DHL, smaller regional players such as Posti or Rhenus, and small
          local players, being present on the market.

(391)     In fact, at the EEA-level, the top 10 providers account for only [10-20]% of the
          contract logistics market, with the biggest provider, DHL, holding [0-5]% of the
          market. The combined market shares of DSV and Schenker at the EEA level will
          be [0-5]%, making the merged entity the second biggest contract logistics provider
          in the EEA.476 Contract logistics markets are also fragmented at the national level.

(392)     The market investigation indicated that customers of contract logistics services
          tend to contract location by location and that the advantages of having a single
          provider across different warehouses are limited. Therefore, larger players compete
          also against smaller local providers At the same time, both competitor and
          customer respondents who expressed a view in the course of market investigation
          considered it important to have a regional network to be successful in winning
          contract logistics contracts from large multinational customers.477 Having an EEA-
          wide network, however, was only considered relevant, rather than important, by the
          majority of respondents who expressed a view.478

5.7.1.2. Closeness of competition

(393)     Contract logistics services are tailor-made, differentiated services, and therefore,
          the suppliers on the market compete with each other to varying degrees depending
          on which value-added services they can provide and on the quality of service they
          can offer. Contract logistics providers of various sizes appear to be able to provide
          adaptations to the services they provide to suit their customers’ needs, as various
          services can be provided within the same warehouse space. In fact, the majority of
          warehouses used for contract logistics are multi-user and designed to serve various
          industries.479 This suggests that closeness of competition is not necessarily linked
          to scale.
