(17)    The Notifying Party considers that the relevant market is that of the wholesale
        distribution of ICT products, including printing consumables, and that a
        segmentation by distribution channels (between ICT wholesalers, wholesalers of
        traditional office supplies and specialised wholesalers) is not appropriate. The
        Parties submit that such a segmentation does not reflect market reality given that
        currently all printing consumables are sold at wholesale level via ICT

(18)    Further, the Notifying Party submits that a segmentation by type of sales (direct

        Notifying Party considers this is also the case for printing consumables.17

        printers could be further segmented into a segment for regular format printers
        which use A3 and A4 paper, on the one hand, and a segment for large format
        printers which function on A2 or larger paper and other supports, on the other
        hand. As regards large format printers, the Commission assessed a further sub-
        segmentation into (i) large format printers for graphic arts and (ii) large format
        printers for computer aided design applications, but ultimately left this question
        open. As regards regular format printers, the Commission considered a further sub-
        segmentation based on (i) functionality, (ii) monochrome (black and white) vs.
        colour printers, (iii) speed, and (iv) page format (A3 vs. A4).37 The Notifying Party
        considers that these segmentations are not appropriate because, from a supply-side
        perspective, manufacturers of printers are able to switch production across the
        various segments and sub-segments. 38

(37)    The Notifying Party also submits that, at the wholesale level, such segmentation

        expected to offer a broad range of ICT products to their customers. Therefore, the
        wholesale distribution of printers is part of a wider market which includes the
        wholesale distribution of all IT products or even all ICT products.39

5.3.1.    Legal framework

(63)      The Horizontal Merger Guidelines52 describe two main ways in which horizontal
          mergers may significantly impede effective competition. In particular, the proposed
          concentration might be creating or strengthening a dominant position: (i) by
          eliminating important competitive constraints on one or more firms, which
          consequently would have increased market power, without resorting to coordinated
          behaviour (non-coordinated effects); or (ii) by changing the nature of competition
          in such a way that firms that previously were not coordinating their behaviour, are
          significantly more likely to coordinate and raise prices or otherwise harm effective
          competition (coordinated effects).

(64)      A merger giving rise to horizontal non-coordinated effects might significantly
          impede effective competition by creating or strengthening the dominant position of
          a single firm, one which, typically, would have an appreciably larger market share
          than the next competitor post-merger. Moreover, also mergers that do not lead to
          the creation of or the strengthening of a single firm’s dominant position may create
          competition concerns under the substantive test set out in Article 2(2) and Article
          2(3) of the Merger Regulation. Regarding mergers in oligopolistic markets, the
          Merger Regulation clarifies that “under certain circumstances, concentrations
          involving the elimination of important competitive constraints that the merging
          parties exerted upon each other, as well as a reduction of competitive pressure on
          the remaining competitors, may, even in the absence of a likelihood of coordination
          between the members of the oligopoly, result in a significant impediment to
          effective competition”.53

(65)      The Horizontal Merger Guidelines list a number of factors which may influence
          whether or not significant horizontal non-coordinated effects are likely to result
          from a merger, such as the large market shares of the merging firms, the fact that
          the merging firms are close competitors, the limited possibilities for customers to
          switch suppliers, or the fact that the merger would eliminate an important
          competitive force. Not all those factors need to be present to make significant non-
          coordinated effects likely and it is not an exhaustive list.54

5.4.1.    Legal framework

(105)     According to the Non-Horizontal Guidelines, in the majority of circumstances,
          conglomerate mergers will not lead to any competition problems.79

(106)     However, foreclosure effects may arise when the combination of products in
          related markets may confer on the merged entity the ability and incentive to
          leverage a strong market position from one market to another closely related
          market by means of tying or bundling or other exclusionary practices. While tying
          and bundling have often no anticompetitive consequences, in certain circumstances
          such practices may lead to a reduction in actual or potential competitors’ ability or
          incentive to compete. This may reduce the competitive pressure on the merged
          entity allowing it to increase prices.80

(107)    In assessing the likelihood of such a scenario, the Commission examines, first,
         whether the merged firm would have the ability to foreclose its competitors,81
         second, whether it would have the economic incentive to do so,82 and, third,
         whether a foreclosure strategy would have a significant detrimental effect on
         competition, thus causing harm to consumers.83 These factors are cumulative and
         often examined together as they are closely intertwined.

(108)    In order to be able to foreclose competitors, the merged entity must have a
         significant degree of market power, which does not necessarily amount to
         dominance, in one of the markets concerned. The effects of bundling or tying can
         only be expected to be substantial when at least one of the merging parties’
         products is viewed by many customers as particularly important and there are few
         relevant alternatives for that product.84 Further, for foreclosure to be a potential
         concern, it must be the case that there is a large common pool of customers, which
         is more likely to be the case when the products are complementary.85 Finally,
         bundling is less likely to lead to foreclosure if rival firms are able to deploy
         effective and timely counter-strategies, such as single-product companies
         combining their offers.86

(109)    The incentive to foreclose competitors through bundling or tying depends on the
         degree to which this strategy is profitable.87 Bundling and tying may entail losses
         or foregone revenues for the merged entity.88 However, they may also allow the
         merged entity to increase profits by gaining market power in the tied goods market,
         protecting market power in the tying good market, or a combination of the two.89

(110)    It is only when a sufficiently large fraction of market output is affected by
         foreclosure resulting from the concentration that the concentration may
         significantly impede effective competition. If there remain effective single-product
         players in either market, competition is unlikely to deteriorate following a
         conglomerate concentration.90 The effect on competition needs to be assessed in
         light of countervailing factors such as the presence of countervailing buyer power
         or the likelihood that entry would maintain effective competition in the upstream or
         downstream markets.91

5.4.3.2.2.     Incentive to foreclose

(126)    As regards the potential bundling of the wholesale distribution of printers and
         printing consumables in Norway or Ireland (considering direct and indirect sales,
         on the one hand, and indirect sales only, on the other hand), even if the Parties were
         considered to have the ability to foreclose rivals (quod non), they would not have
         the incentive to engage in such a foreclosure strategy.

(127)    This is because the increments in the market for the wholesale distribution of
         printing consumables in Norway or Ireland (considering direct and indirect sales,
         on the one hand, and indirect sales only, on the other hand) are minimal (between
         [0-5]%-[0-5]% and the delta of the HHI is below 150).

(128)    For the sake of completeness, the increments in the market for the wholesale
         distribution of printing consumables in the Nordic region, Denmark, Sweden
         (considering indirect sales only in those potential geographic markets) are also
         minimal (between [0-5]%-[0-5]% and the delta of the HHI is below 150). With
         regards to France (considering indirect sales only), as set out above in Section
         5.3.3.2, the increment of [10-20]% is relatively modest and there are several
         alternative wholesale distributors of printing consumables.

(129)    Therefore, the Transaction does not significantly alter the merged entitity’s
         incentives in these pre-existing conglomerate relationships.

5.4.4.   Conclusion

(130)    In view of the above considerations and in light of the results of the market
         investigation and the evidence and information available to it, the Commission
         concludes that the Transaction would not raise serious doubts as to its compatibility
         with the internal market as a result of conglomerate effects, notably by bundling
         the wholesale distribution of printers and printing consumables, considering that
         the merged entity would not have the ability and/or the incentive to foreclose its
         rivals.
