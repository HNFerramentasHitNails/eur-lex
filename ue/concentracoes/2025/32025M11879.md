4.2.1.2. The Notifying Party’s view
(13)    The Notifying Party agrees with the Commission’s previous decisions in which it
        segmented the market for the supply of printers into separate markets for the supply of
        regular and large format printers respectively (15), and further segmented the market for
        the supply of regular format printers into separate markets for the supply of branded and
        unbranded regular format printers respectively (16).
(14)    The Notifying Party submits that, considering the technological advancements since the
        Commission’s previous decisions, the market segmentations identified in earlier cases
        (such as single-function versus multi-function printers, based on print speed, mono
        versus colour, and A3 versus A4 format printers) no longer constitute meaningful
        distinctions for the purposes of segmenting the market for the supply of branded regular
        format printers (17). In particular:
        (a)    Single-function and multi-function printers: The Notifying Party does not
               consider it appropriate or correct to segment between single-function and multi-
               function printers. In particular, the Notifying Party submits that from a demand
               side perspective, pricing of single-function and multi-function printers is broadly
               the same. Further, from a supply side perspective, suppliers typically use the same
               engine for their single-function and multi-function printers. Similarly, most
               suppliers supply both single-function and multi-function printers (18).

4.2.2.1. Commission precedents
(21)      In previous decisions, the Commission noted that there are strong indications that the
          market for branded regular format printers is wider than national or even EEA-wide.
          Ultimately, the Commission left the geographic market definition for branded regular
          format printers open (28).

4.2.2.2. The Notifying Party’s view
(22)      The Notifying Party submits that the relevant geographic market for the supply of
          branded regular format printers should be defined as at least EEA-wide and potentially
          worldwide in scope because (i) the Parties are active across the EEA and even globally,
          where they compete with other global suppliers; (ii) customers source their printers on
          an international basis; (iii) some vendors, [details about distribution networks] have a
          single distribution centre from which it supplies the entire EEA; (iv) product
          characteristics do not vary materially throughout the world and (v) no material trade
          barriers exist. Within the EEA, vendors’ suggested sales process do not vary materially
          between member states(29).

5.3.      Horizontal unilateral effects

5.3.1.    Legal framework
(35)      The Horizontal Merger Guidelines (35) describe two main ways in which horizontal
          mergers may significantly impede effective competition. In particular, the proposed
          concentration might be creating or strengthening a dominant position: (i) by eliminating
          important competitive constraints on one or more firms, which consequently would
          have increased market power, without resorting to coordinated behaviour (non-
          coordinated effects); and (ii) by changing the nature of competition in such a way that
          firms that previously were not coordinating their behaviour, are significantly more
          likely to coordinate and raise prices or otherwise harm effective competition
          (coordinated effects).
(36)      A merger giving rise to horizontal non-coordinated effects might significantly impede
          effective competition by creating or strengthening the dominant position of a single
          firm, one which, typically, would have an appreciably larger market share than the next
          competitor post-merger. Moreover, also mergers that do not lead to the creation of or
          the strengthening of a single firm’s dominant position may create competition concerns
          under the substantive test set out in Article 2(2) and Article 2(3) of the Merger
          Regulation. Regarding mergers in oligopolistic markets, the Merger Regulation clarifies
          that “under certain circumstances, concentrations involving the elimination of
          important competitive constraints that the merging parties exerted upon each other, as
          well as a reduction of competitive pressure on the remaining competitors, may, even in

          in a significant impediment to effective competition” (36).
(37)      The Horizontal Merger Guidelines list a number of factors which may influence
          whether or not significant horizontal non-coordinated effects are likely to result from a
          merger, such as the large market shares of the merging firms, the fact that the merging
          firms are close competitors, the limited possibilities for customers to switch suppliers,
          or the fact that the merger would eliminate an important competitive force. Not all those
          factors need to be present to make significant non-coordinated effects likely and it is
          not an exhaustive list (37).
