4.1.1.2. The Notifying Party’s view
(16)    The Notifying Party considers, in line with the approach of the Commission, (12)
        that sugar confectionery should be distinguished from chocolate confectionery.
(17)    The Notifying Party further submits that the relevant market is the manufacture
        and sale of sugar confectionery products, encompassing both hard and soft
        candies. (13) The Notifying Party considers that the different product types
        included in sugar confectionery – e.g., jellies, gummies, hard boiled candy,
        dragées, or mixes – present different degrees of “hardness” but do not fall into
        separate relevant markets, because consumers typically perceive sugar
        confectionery as a single, broad category of ‘candies’, from which they choose
        based on flavour, packaging, or occasion, rather than texture or consistency.
(18)    The Notifying Party further submits that a segmentation between branded and
        private label products is not warranted, as both categories strongly compete with
        each other across the value chain. (14)
(19)    The Notifying Party finally submits that a segmentation by sales channels is not
        warranted either because, while contractual terms or order sizes may differ, the
        underlying products, packaging formats (apart from logistical adjustments), and
        pricing principles in one channel do not differ materially from those applied to
        other sales channels. (15)

4.1.2.    Geographic market decision

4.1.2.1. The Commission’s decisional practice
(30)      In previous decisions, the Commission considered that the markets for the
          manufacture and sale of sugar confectionery products (and their possible
          segmentations) were national in scope. (30)

4.1.2.2. The Notifying Party’s view
(31)      The Notifying Party considers that the market for the manufacture and supply of
          sugar confectionery is at least national in scope, (31) but in a context that is
          becoming increasingly internationalised, harmonised and “Europeanised” due to
          the following factors: (i) minor transport cost impact compared to raw materials
          and labour; (ii) importance of European trademarks fostering EU-wide brand
          creation; and (iii) presence of large manufacturers with multiple EU production
          sites.
(32)      The Notifying Party nonetheless accepts that the European Commission may
          prefer a more conservative approach and define the market as national in scope
          based on the following factors: taste (vary by country; specific products cater to
          national demand), market shares (different across EU Member States), national
          trademarks (predominantly national, not EU-wide), distribution (primarily
          through national retail distributors), negotiations (conducted on a national level),
          pricing (set nationally; varies between countries), freshness (proximity to
          distribution required for product freshness).

4.1.2.3. The Commission’s assessment
(33)      During the market investigation, the majority of retailers indicated that they
          consider the conditions of competition (e.g. same suppliers, same quality, same
          prices) to be similar at a national level and that they generally purchase products
          at national level. A market participant explained for instance that “Each country
          will have different levels of retail prices which lead to different levels of
          competition.” (32)

5.1.1.    Legal Framework
(63)      The Horizontal Merger Guidelines distinguish two main ways in which mergers
          between actual or potential competitors on the same relevant market may
          significantly impede effective competition, namely non-coordinated effects and
          coordinated effects. (64)
(64)      Non-coordinated effects may significantly impede effective competition by
          eliminating the competitive constraint imposed by one merging party on the other,
          as a result of which the merged entity would have increased market power without
          resorting to coordinated behaviour. According to recital 25 of the Merger
          Regulation, a significant impediment to effective competition can result from the
          anticompetitive effects of a concentration even if the merged entity would not
          have a dominant position on the market concerned. In this regard, the Horizontal
          Merger Guidelines consider not only the direct loss of competition between the
          merging firms, but also the reduction in competitive pressure on non-merging
          firms in the same market that could be brought about by the merger. (65)
(65)      The Horizontal Merger Guidelines list a number of factors, which may influence
          the rise of substantial non-coordinated effects from a merger, such as: the large
          market shares of the merging firms; the fact that the merging firms are close
          competitors; the limited possibilities for customers to switch suppliers; or the fact
          that the merger would eliminate an important competitive force. The list of factors
          also applies if a merger would create or strengthen a dominant position or would
          otherwise significantly impede effective competition due to non-coordinated
          effects. Furthermore, not all of those factors need to be present to make significant
          non-coordinated effects likely and the list itself is not an exhaustive list. (66)

5.2.3.1. Bundling and tying
(102)     Based on the evidence in the Commission’s file, including the results of the
          market investigation, the Commission considers that post-Transaction Ferrero
          will not have the ability to engage in tying and bundling strategies of products
          across its portfolio in order to foreclose rivals.
(103)     Ferrero enjoys a very high market share in chocolate spreadable products in
          Belgium ([50-60]% including private labels and [70-80]% excluding private
          labels) and in France ([60-70]% including private labels and [60-70]% excluding
          private labels). However, the market shares of CPK in the product markets where
          it is active in these countries are considerably lower. CPK’s highest market shares
          in Belgium and France, which are in the market for the manufacture and sale of
          soft candies, are below 25%. CPK’s market share in Belgium in this market is
          [10-20]% including private labels and [20-30]% excluding private labels; in
          France is [10-20]% including private labels and [10-20]% excluding private
          labels.
(104)     Thus, the risk that the new entity would be able to leverage its market position
          into the markets where CPK is active seems low.
(105)     First, internal documents provided by Ferrero concerning negotiations with
          retailers confirm that negotiations are carried out by product category, reflecting
          the retailers’ internal organisation. (112) Consequently, products of different
          categories such as chocolate confectionery, sugar confectionary, sweet spreadable
          products, biscuits, or ice-cream would not be negotiated together. The separation
          of negotiations with retailers by category would prevent tying or bundling
          practices (especially of a lasting nature) across product categories.
(106)     Second, the majority of retailers that replied to the Commission’s questionnaire
          confirmed that Ferrero does not require retailers to purchase one Ferrero product
          in other to be able to buy another Ferrero product. (113)

         through tying or bundling. Therefore, the Commission does not need to discuss
         the existence of the incentive for Ferrero to foreclose rivals by engaging in tying
         and bundling strategies and the anticompetitive effects of these potential
         strategies.

5.2.3.2. Portfolio Leveraging
(109)    During the Commission’s investigation, some market participants pointed to the
         fact that the Transaction would reinforce Ferrero’s portfolio, which it could use
         in the negotiations with retailers to pass on price increases and acquire additional
         shelf space. These concerns highlight that, at the beginning of negotiation cycles,
         Ferrero typically presents a price proposal across product categories with limited
         scope for negotiations and that Ferrero would limit or stop supplies if retailers do
         not agree to its prices. (117)
(110)    However, as detailed below, the evidence in the Commission’s file and the results
         of the market investigation indicate that the Transaction would not give Ferrero
         additional market power or significantly change the negotiation dynamics with
         retailers.
(111)    First, CPK’s market shares remain below 30% (including and excluding private
         labels) in all markets in which CPK is active in Belgium and France. In line with
         Non-Horizontal Merger Guidelines, the Commission is unlikely to raise concerns
         about non-horizontal mergers where the market share of one of the Parties remains
         below 30%. (118) Thus, the market power acquired by Ferrero as a result of the
         Transaction in markets closely related to its activities in Belgium and France
         remains limited. As a consequence, the Transaction will not result in a change to
         Ferrero’s negotiation dynamics with retailers.
