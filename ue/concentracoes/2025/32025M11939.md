        investment generally linked to the volume of milk supplied. (13) In return the
        member farmer shares in the cooperative’s profits and is represented in the latter’s
        decision-making bodies. By contrast, when supplying raw milk to a private dairy
        company no equity investment is required, nor is there any form of profit-sharing.
        Second, the member farmer commits to supply all its milk to the cooperative.
        Conversely, the cooperative is obliged to accept and process all raw milk
        produced by its member farmers. (14) By contrast, in case of sale of raw milk to a
        private dairy company, the volumes and the duration of the supply agreement are
        the subject of a contractual agreement between the parties.
(12)    Further, a dairy cooperative cannot unilaterally end its relationship with a member

        or other governing documentation, and can switch to another cooperative or
        decide to start supplying to a private dairy company. (15)

         irrespective of the extent of such overlap. In the Commission’s view this approach
         does not correctly reflect the extent of the competitive constraint exerted by rival
         plants (which does depend on the extent of the overlap between the two plants’
         respective catchment areas).
(138)    Furthermore, the Parties made an exception for two relatively large rival
         plants (157), which in the Parties’ view source milk from more distant areas than
         the typical plant. To reflect this, the Parties included the sales of these two rival
         plants also in the market shares calculations for catchment areas for which, based
         on the typical 80% driving time, the two rivals’ plants would not overlap with the
         catchment area around the Parties’ plant. While the Commission acknowledges
         that procurement conditions may vary depending on factors such as facility size
         and location, the Parties did not sufficiently substantiate this adjustment.
         Moreover, the Parties did not apply this exception consistently across all rivals.
         In particular, the opposite might occur, where smaller rival facilities may not even
         overlap with the Parties’ catchment areas if they are able to meet their needs from
         closer sources. The Parties submit alternative scenarios seeking to account for
         these considerations.

5.6.2.    Legal framework
(249)     Pursuant to the Commission Guidelines on the assessment of non-horizontal
          mergers under the Council Regulation on the control of concentrations between
          undertakings (the ‘Non-Horizontal Merger Guidelines’) (312) a vertical merger may
          result in anticompetitive foreclosure effects where actual or potential rivals’
          access to supplies or markets is hampered or eliminated as a result of the merger,
          thereby reducing these companies’ ability and/or incentive to compete. Such
          foreclosure may discourage entry or expansion of rivals or encourage their exit.
          Foreclosure thus can be found even if the foreclosed rivals are not forced to exit
          the market: it is sufficient that the rivals are disadvantaged and consequently led
          to compete less effectively. (313)
(250)     More specifically, the Non-Horizontal Merger Guidelines distinguish between
          two main ways in which vertical mergers may significantly impede effective
          competition, namely input foreclosure and customer foreclosure.
          (a)    Input foreclosure arises where, post-merger, the new entity would be likely
                 to restrict access to the products or services that it would have otherwise
                 supplied absent the merger, thereby raising its actual or potential
                 downstream rivals’ costs by making it harder for them to obtain supplies of
                 the input under similar prices and conditions as absent the merger. (314) For

5.7.2.    Legal framework
(292)     According to the Non-Horizontal Guidelines, in the majority of circumstances,
          conglomerate mergers will not lead to any competition problems. (342)
(293)     However, foreclosure effects may arise when the combination of products in
          related markets may confer on the combined entity the ability and incentive to
          leverage a strong market position from one market to another closely related
          market by means of tying or bundling or other exclusionary practices. While tying
          and bundling have often no anticompetitive consequences, in certain
          circumstances such practices may lead to a reduction in actual or potential
          competitors' ability or incentive to compete. This may reduce the competitive
          pressure on the combined entity allowing it to increase prices. (343)
(294)     In assessing the likelihood of such a scenario, the Commission examines, first,
          whether the merged firm would have the ability to foreclose its competitors (344),
          second, whether it would have the economic incentive to do so (345) and, third,
          whether a foreclosure strategy would have a significant detrimental effect on
          competition, thus causing harm to consumers. (346) These factors are cumulative
          and often examined together as they are closely intertwined.
(295)     In order to be able to foreclose competitors, the combined entity must have a
          significant degree of market power, which does not necessarily amount to
          dominance, in one of the markets concerned. The effects of bundling or tying can
          only be expected to be substantial when at least one of the merging parties’
          products is viewed by many customers as particularly important and there are few
          relevant alternatives for that product. (347) Further, for foreclosure to be a potential
          concern, it must be the case that there is a large common pool of customers, which
          is more likely to be the case when the products are complementary. (348) Finally,
          bundling is less likely to lead to foreclosure if rival firms are able to deploy

          in Belgium, such as Loicq, De Kazerij (Flandrien) and De Moerenaar, as seen in
          Table 38.
(302)     Second, the Parties did not conduct a bundling or a tying strategy in the past three
          years. Modern retailers that expressed an opinion during the market investigation
          confirmed that in the past three years they have not been offered a bundled deal,
          where two or more dairy products were sold together at a discounted price, by
          FrieslandCampina nor by Milcobel, except for one instance where Milcobel
          offered a bundled deal to one modern retailer which turned it down. (356)
(303)     Moreover, modern retailers that expressed an opinion during the market
          investigation indicated that, in the past three years, they have not been required to
          purchase one or several of FrieslandCampina’s products nor of Milcobel’s
          products to be able to buy another product of the same company. (357)
(304)     Third, the majority of modern retailers that expressed an opinion during the
          market investigation indicated that the combined entity will not become a more
          interesting supplier given its larger portfolio of products, as they already have
          access to the Parties’ entire range of product. (358)
(305)     Fourth, should the Parties conduct a bundling or a tying strategy following the
          Transaction, customers could retaliate against such strategy, as the results of the
          market investigation indicated that the Parties do not supply any product that
          could not be replaced by modern retailers from an alternative supplier. Modern
          retailers could then easily turn to alternative suppliers to replace the Parties’
          products.

          players controlling more than 75% of the Belgian market. (364)
(309)     Based on the above, the Commission concludes that the combined entity will not
          have the ability to conduct any bundling or tying strategies between, on the one
          hand, Belgian Gouda and, on the other hand, Dutch Gouda in the modern retail
          channel.

5.7.5.    Conclusion
(310)     In view of the above, the Commission concludes that the Transaction does not
          raise serious doubts as to its compatibility with the internal market or a substantial
          part of it as a result of the conglomerate relationship between the Parties’ activities
          in the supply of Belgian Gouda to the modern retail channel on the one hand, and
          the Parties’ activities in the supply of Dutch Gouda to the modern retail channel
          on the other hand.
