4.1.      Product market definition
(8)       Methanol is a basic liquid petrochemical, manufactured using, primarily, natural
          gas and coal as a feedstock. Methanol is mainly used in the production of other
          commodity chemicals (such as formaldehyde or acetic acid) that are then used to
          make raw materials for construction and automotive parts, paints, paper, plastics
          etc. In addition, methanol is used for various fuel applications and the production
          of olefins used in the manufacture of chemical and polymer products (such as
          polyethylene and polypropylene). (9)
(9)       Based on the feedstock used in its production, methanol can be referred to as either
          grey (non-renewable natural gas), green (renewable natural gas, biomass and
          green hydrogen), blue (non-renewable feedstock with an integrated carbon
          capture), or brown (coal).
(10)      While both Parties are active in the production and supply of grey and green
          methanol, Methanex’ sales of green methanol are negligible (representing [less
          than 5%] of its output). Neither Methanex nor OCI’s Methanol Business supply
          any “blue” or “brown” methanol in the EEA or worldwide. (10)

4.2.      Geographic market definition

4.2.1.    The Commission’s past practice
(19)      In its past practice, the Commission considered that, although methanol is
          internationally traded as a commodity (as it can be profitably transported over
          long distances), the market is not worldwide in scope as conditions of competition
          vary between the three main demand areas of the world (Western Europe, North
          America and Asia). (25) Consequently, in previous decisions, the Commission has
          conducted its assessment at the EEA level. (26)

4.2.2.    The Notifying Party’s views
(20)      The Notifying Party submits that the geographic scope of the market for methanol
          should be considered global instead of regional because: (i) methanol is sourced
          and supplied globally and can be easily transported; (ii) following the higher
          natural gas prices in the EEA since 2021, European production of methanol is
          largely idled and the majority of the EEA methanol demand is imported from e.g.,
          Trinidad and Tobago, the US, Equatorial Guinea, Egypt and Venezuela,
          evidencing global competition; (iii) transportation costs (currently approximately
          [10-20]% of the price) are not sufficient to prevent significant imports of methanol
          into the EEA; (iv) there are no material regulatory hurdles for the import of
          methanol into Europe; and (v) there are correlations between prices across
          regions. (27)
(21)      However, the Notifying Party considers that, for the purposes of the present
          Transaction, the exact geographic market definition can be left open given that no
          competition concerns arise regardless of whether the relevant market is
          maintained to be EEA-wide or broader (e.g., global) in scope. (28)

(82)     While methanol is a homogenous product, the structure of the EEA methanol
         market is not conducive to reaching a coordinated outcome, for the following
         reasons.
(83)     First, the market for the supply of methanol in the EEA is fragmented, (160) with
         numerous active suppliers (see Table 1 above). Post-Transaction, the combined
         market share of Methanex and OCI’s Methanol Business would remain relatively
         moderate, estimated at around [20-40]%. (161) The market investigation
         confirmed that there will remain a sufficient number of credible alternative
         suppliers of methanol to the EEA post-Transaction as explained in paragraph (52).
         Even if the Transaction results in a reduction of the number of competitors on the
         market, at least six credible competitors that are larger than or similar in size to
         OCI’s Methanol Business will remain. Generally, the higher the number of firms,
         the more complex it is to reach a common understanding on the terms of
         coordination. (162)

         overcapacity as explained in more detail in paragraph (59) , which means that
         suppliers are likely incentivised to seek to increase their sales to customers.
         Moreover, there are low transportation barriers, which facilitate arbitrage
         opportunities across regions, including from outside the EEA. Other suppliers

5.3.2.5. Deterrence mechanisms
(99)     There are reasons to consider that market conditions for the supply of methanol
         to the EEA do not support the existence of effective deterrence mechanisms that
         could sustain coordination over time.
(100)    In particular, contracts are typically annual and (at least in the case of some
         suppliers) tend to be renegotiated towards the end of the year, meaning that
         suppliers wanting to respond to deviation may need to wait a year before being
         able to effectively respond. (187) Moreover, it may be challenging to target one
         particular supplier with retaliation, given that customers tend to multi-source and
         seek quotes from multiple suppliers. It is unclear to what extent it would be
         credible for suppliers to enter into a price war as a form of retaliation or
         deterrence, given that the EEA market for the supply of methanol is overall
         competitive and prone to competitive pressure from imports as explained in
         paragraph (25), meaning that once prices stabilise, it is not obvious that losses
         could be recouped. The market investigation and evidence on file did not provide
         any reason to consider the Transaction would increase the effectiveness of any
         deterrence mechanisms.
(101)    The Commission does not, however, need to conclude as to whether the threat of
         future retaliation in this case would be sufficient to sustain a coordinated strategy.
         This is because market features do not support the possibility of reaching terms
         of coordination (Section 5.3.2.3), the relevant market is not transparent and it
         would not be feasible to monitor deviations (Section 5.3.2.4), the reactions of
         outsiders could jeopardise coordination (Section 5.3.2.5), and the Transaction
         does not materially increase the likelihood of coordination (as explained in these
         Sections).

5.3.3.   Conclusion
(108)    In conclusion, the Transaction is unlikely to increase the likelihood that suppliers
         of methanol in the EEA are able to coordinate their behaviour. This is because
         market features do not support the possibility of reaching terms of coordination
         (Section 5.3.2.3), the relevant market is not transparent and it would not be
         feasible to monitor deviations (Section 5.3.2.4), the reactions of outsiders could
         jeopardise coordination (Section 5.3.2.5), and the Transaction does not materially
         increase the likelihood of coordination (as explained in these Sections).
(109)    In light of the foregoing, the Commission concludes that the Transaction does not
         raise serious doubts as to its compatibility with the internal market from the risk
         of coordinated effects with respect to the supply of methanol in the EEA.
