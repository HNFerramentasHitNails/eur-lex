4.1.1.1.3.1.   Personal luxury goods vs. mass-market fashion goods
(15)     The Commission’s market investigation confirmed the distinction between
         personal luxury goods and mass-market fashion goods. (20) A large majority of
         brands responding to the market investigation considered that personal luxury
         goods and mass-market fashion goods are not substitutable. For instance, one
         brand noted that “While both categories may fulfill [sic] the functional need for
         clothing or accessories, they serve very different consumer motivations. For this
         reason, they are not generally viewed as substitutes by consumers. Customers
         purchasing luxury goods often do so for reasons beyond utility — such as prestige,
         identity, or investment value — whereas mass-market shoppers are typically
         driven by practicality, trend orientation, and affordability.” (21) Retail
         competitors also noted that the environment in which personal luxury goods are
         sold represent a prestige of a brand and limited volume or exceptional design:
         “Often there are limited collections or collaborations with a small quantity
         offered compared to mass market products. Retail prices are higher then [sic]
         mass market products because of the brand image and its desired products. Some
         luxury products can be very extraordinary and made out of special and expensive
         raw materials”. (22)
(16)     In line with its past precedent, the Commission concludes that mass-market
         fashion goods form a separate market from personal luxury goods and therefore
         will conduct its competitive assessment on a retail market for mass-market
         fashion goods which excludes personal luxury goods.

(32)     The Commission’s market investigation indicates that the multi-category
         e-commerce players which in addition to mass-market fashion goods also sell
         non-fashion consumer goods such as electronics, kitchen, beauty, personal care
         and similar items (for instance, Amazon or Bol.com) appear to compete with
         multi-brand mass-market fashion goods retailers. Therefore, the Commission
         considers that despite their broader product focus such multi-category
         e-commerce platforms should not be distinguished into a separate segment.
(33)     First, multi-category e-commerce platforms offer a large variety of different
         fashion brands, including some of those available on the Parties’ multi-brand
         platforms that have a clear mass-market fashion focus. (48) While it appears that
         some brands may prefer to sell through specialised online stores that focus on the
         fashion segment, (49) a number of everyday mass-market and conventional
         leisure-focused brands are available on multi-category e-commerce
         platforms. (50)
(34)     Second, the Parties’ internal documents suggest that consumers view multi-
         category e-commerce platforms as credible alternatives to multi-brand mass-
         market retailers. Zalando’s consumer surveys consistently show that [details of
         the Parties’ internal documents]. For instance, one internal document shows that
         [details of the Parties’ internal documents]. (51) In another internal document,
         Zalando noted that [details of the Parties’ internal documents]. (52)
(35)     Third, on the one hand, the multi-category e-commerce platforms offer consumers
         somewhat similar online interface (e.g. search possibilities and/or brand
         presentation), services and overall shopping experience. For instance, consumers
         are able to browse through different brands on a single website, request to ship
         goods EEA-wide/ fast delivery and have similar return policies. On the other
         hand, the majority of brands and competitors responding to the market

5.       COMPETITIVE ASSESSMENT

5.1.     Legal Framework
(97)     Under Article 2(2) and (3) of the Merger Regulation, the Commission must assess
         whether a proposed concentration would significantly impede effective
         competition in the internal market or in a substantial part of it, in particular
         through the creation or strengthening of a dominant position. In this respect, a
         merger can entail horizontal (non-coordinated and/or coordinated) and/or non-
         horizontal effects (in this case due to conglomerate links).
(98)     Horizontal effects: The Guidelines on the assessment of horizontal mergers
         (‘Horizontal Merger Guidelines’) (123) describe two main ways in which
         horizontal mergers may significantly impede effective competition. In particular,
         the proposed concentration might be creating or strengthening a dominant
         position: (i) by eliminating important competitive constraints on one or more
         firms, which consequently would have increased market power, without resorting
         to coordinated behaviour (non-coordinated effects); and (ii) by changing the
         nature of competition in such a way that firms that previously were not
         coordinating their behaviour, are significantly more likely to coordinate and raise
         prices or otherwise harm effective competition (coordinated effects). A merger
         giving rise to horizontal non-coordinated effects might significantly impede
         effective competition by creating or strengthening the dominant position of a
         single firm, one which, typically, would have an appreciably larger market share
         than the next competitor post-merger. Moreover, also mergers that do not lead to
         the creation of or the strengthening of a single firm’s dominant position may
         create competition concerns under the substantive test set out in Article 2(2) and
         Article 2(3) of the Merger Regulation.

         leverage a strong market position from one market to another closely related

         circumstances such practices may lead to a reduction in actual or potential
         competitors' ability or incentive to compete. This may reduce the competitive
         pressure on the merged entity allowing it to increase prices. (127)

         whether it would have the economic incentive to do so, (129) and, third, whether a
         foreclosure strategy would have a significant detrimental effect on competition,
         thus causing harm to consumers. (130) In practice, these factors are often examined
         together as they are closely intertwined.

5.3.2.4. Barriers to entry and expansion
(192)    The results of the market investigation show that there are relatively high entry
         barriers to the market for online retail supply of multi-brand multi-product mass-
         market fashion goods. (258) In particular, respondents have indicated development
         of brand awareness and establishing brand relationships as the most difficult
         obstacles to overcome for a new entrant. (259) For instance, one competitor
         summarised “while the technical entry barriers may appear moderate, the
         commercial, relational, and financial barriers are substantial. Success requires
         capital, industry access, and brand partnerships, making this market difficult to
         enter for newcomers without significant backing and expertise.” (260) Such issues
         were also recognised by some of the brands. For example, one brand explained
         that “[b]uilding trust with strong brands is extremely difficult for new entrants.
         Most brands already have established partnerships with major platforms (like
         Zalando and About You), and they are cautious about taking risks with new,
         unproven platforms”. (261) Developing a website was generally considered to be
         relatively easy by most respondents in light of the availability of third-party
         providers. (262) In terms of logistics, the views were more mixed. On one hand
         respondents noted that third-part logistics providers are widely available, whereas
         on the other they considered that setting up an efficient logistics network can be
         complex. (263)
(193)    In terms of expansion across the EEA for an online retailer already supplying
         mass-market fashion goods in some EEA countries, the market investigation
         results indicate that such barriers are more moderate. (264) However, in particular
         competing retailers note that certain obstacles also hinder the expansion within
         the EEA. For instance, one competitor explained that “geographic expansion is
         feasible, but requires targeted investments and localized strategies—making it a
         moderately complex endeavor, especially for mid-sized or niche players”. (265)
         Further, as described by a brand: “For an existing company, expanding
         technically into another country is not a major challenge per se. The necessary
         logistical requirements in particular have already been met; all that is needed is
         an adaptation in the shop, in the carrier network and in the payment area. The
         challenges here are also the financial resources required to conquer market share
         in a country. Depending on the diversification/saturation of the market, higher or
         lower financial investments are required.” (266)

5.3.2.7. Consumers’ ability to switch
(226)     The market investigation confirms that there are no significant barriers for a
          consumer to switch between online suppliers of mass-market fashion goods. The
          Commission’s market investigation did not indicate that there would be any
          significant differences in the consumers’ ability to switch depending on the
          geographic market considered as the elements driving the ability to switch are
          similar in nature across the EEA and there are alternatives available in each EEA
          country.
(227)     The majority of both competing retailers and brands indicated that consumers
          regularly shop across retailers/platforms, in particular in search for better
          offers. (328) One respondent explained that “[i]n our experience, consumers
          typically cross-shop across multiple retailers and platforms rather than
          remaining loyal to just one. This behavior is particularly common in the online
          mass-market fashion segment, where price sensitivity, assortment variety, and
          convenience play major roles in purchase decisions. Consumers often compare
          prices, product availability, delivery times, and return policies across platforms
          like Zalando, About You, ASOS, Amazon Fashion, and others before making a
          purchase. Loyalty is relatively low, especially when the same or similar products
          are available from multiple sources. The widespread use of price comparison
          tools, search aggregators, and promotional campaigns further encourages this
          cross-shopping behavior.” (329)
(228)     Moreover, as described in Section 5.3.2.2, the Commission finds that there are
          various other players active on the market to whom mass-market fashion
          consumers could turn to. Furthermore, as discussed in Section 5.3.2.4, there is an
          appreciable level of substitutability across the various distribution channels
          allowing consumers to purchase mass-market fashion goods not only from
          suppliers within the market, but also from various other distribution channels,
          such as mono-brand/DTC websites, without occurring any meaningful switching
          costs.
(229)     In light of the above, the Commission finds that there are no substantial barriers
          for a consumer to switch between online multi-brand, multi-product retailers of
          mass-market fashion goods.
