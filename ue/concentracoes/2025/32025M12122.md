4.2.1.    Product Market Definition

4.2.1.1. The Commission’s past decisional practice

(16)      In its prior decisional practice, the Commission considered a distinct product
          market for contract foodservices (which is separate from that of concession
          foodservices) ( 22 ) for the “provision of foodservice[s] outside the home,
          performed by third parties [i.e., outsourced], typically on the premises of public
          or private sector clients and involving the supply of food and drink to customers
          for whom that service is not the primary reason for their presence on the
          premises”. (23)

(17)      The Commission left open whether the market for the provision of contract
          foodservices should be further segmented according to sectors in which the
          customers of contract foodservices are active, for example B&I, Healthcare,
          Education, and DOR. (24)

4.2.1.2. The Notifying Party’s views

(18)      The Notifying Party does not contest the existence of a distinct market for the
          provision of contract foodservices. (25)

(19)      However, it considers that the contract foodservices market should include both
          in-house ( 26 ) and outsourced supply, because in-house supply constitutes a
          relevant competitive constraint when a customer is assessing whether to outsource
          for the first time and on subsequent renewals of an outsourced contract. (27)

(20)      The Notifying Party also considers that the market for the provision of contract
          foodservices should not be further segmented based on the customers’ industry or
          activities, due to high supply-side substitutability and a high level of commonality
          in customer demand from different industry sectors. (28)

(28)     On the other hand, the Commission’s market investigation shows that in practice
         there is very limited substitution between the two services. For example, large
         competitors in the Netherlands and in Germany explained that they have neither
         experienced instances where customers originally outsourcing foodservices opted
         to insource them, nor have they experienced customers threatening foodservice
         providers with insourcing as a negotiation tactic. ( 36 ) The competitors’ view,

         expressed a view in the Commission’s market investigation. (37) This is also in

         contestable market, consisting of contracted services that are being out-sourced

         foodservices might present an opportunity for expansion for the Parties and their
         competitors, they cannot be considered as part of the market for the provision of
         contract foodservices for the purposes of the competitive assessment. This is
         because they do not constitute direct competitors to the foodservice providers and
         their in-house services are not actually contestable by foodservice providers.
         Therefore, the inclusion of these entities in the relevant market could lead to the
         underestimation of the competitive position of foodservice providers in the
         currently addressable market.

                foodservices in the B&I sector between “premium” and “standard” services

(35)     On the basis of the results of the Commission’s market investigation and of some
         other evidence available in the Commission’s file the Commission has considered
         a possible segmentation of the market for the provision of contract foodservices
         to B&I customers between “premium” (or white-collar) and “standard” (or blue-
         collar) services. (44)

(36)     On the one hand, competitors active in the Netherlands explained that there are
         differences in the meal expectations and spending capabilities within the B&I
         segment, not only between business venues and industrial sites, but also between
         types of business customers.

(37)     A large competitor of the Parties in the Netherlands explained that it “tailors its
         offers depending on the tender it participates in (e.g., menu planning; quality;
         price; choice of food; plant-based options on the menu), which is contingent upon
         specific client needs. In this context, [the competitor] believes that there is a
         distinction between premium and non-premium offering. By way of example it
         explains that a bank employee can dedicate more time to lunch, will have space
         to sit at its companies’ premises, is likely to work whilst eating and/or take
         advantage of lunch time to socialise with other employees, will expect better
         quality lunch (often more plant-based options) and will be willing to pay a higher
         amount for a better-quality meal. On the contrary, an industrial worker, will have
         less time and space to eat, and will be more price sensitive. Thus, the offering
         differs significantly within the B&I segment, based clients’ needs. In this context,
         [the competitor] positions itself as a more value proposition (i.e., stronger on non-
         premium [standard])”. (45) Another large competitor in the Netherlands confirmed
         that “[t]he requirements of the businesses comprising this market segment [B&I]
         are similar. However, there are differences between the expectations and
         spending of business customers (referred to as “white-collar”) and industrial site
         / factory customers (referred to as “blue-collar”). In particular, the employees of
         white-collar businesses are willing to spend more and eat healthier and smaller
         portions of food. Therefore, the required volumes are lower, and profit margins
         are higher. On the contrary, the blue-collar employees prefer to spend less and
         eat larger portions of food. Therefore, the market is volume based, and profit
         margins are lower. In this context, [the competitor] considers that the B&I market
         could be further segmented between white- and blue-collar businesses”. (46)
