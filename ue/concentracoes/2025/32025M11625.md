5.2.    Assessment

(15)    PostNL falls under the Large Company Regime in Dutch law.16 In general, the
        Large Company Regime imposes an obligation on a company to install a
        Supervisory Board and provides for a shift of certain powers from the company's
        Shareholders’ Meeting to the Supervisory Board.17

(16)    The question whether VESA de facto controls PostNL raises two sub-questions.
        First, whether in view of the attendance patterns in PostNL’s Shareholders’
        Meetings, VESA is highly likely to achieve a majority at the Shareholders’
        Meeting with a stake of 27.73%.18 As discussed in section 5.2.1 below, EPCG
        considers that this is the case and the Commission agrees. However, given the
        somehow limited powers of the Shareholders’ Meeting under the Large Company
        Regime, a subsequent question is whether VESA can in fact exercise decisive
        influence on PostNL with the stable majority that it enjoys at the Shareholder’s
        Meeting. As explained further in section 5.2.2 below the Commission considers
        that through its majority in the Shareholders’ Meeting, VESA can exercise decisive
        influence on PostNL.

(21)      The daily business of PostNL is managed by its Board of Management.22 The

5.2.2.2. Analysis

(26)    Paragraph 57 CJN provides that control may also be exercised by a minority
        shareholder who is entitled to ‘manage the activities of the company and determine
        his business policy on the basis of the organisational structure’. According to
        paragraph 69 CJN, veto rights concerning decisions on the appointment and
        dismissal of senior management, such as members of the board, are very important
        for the determination of control and usually confer upon the holder the power to
        exercise decisive influence on the commercial policy of an undertaking.

(27)    The Supervisory Board of PostNL, has important management functions. In
        particular, PostNL’s Supervisory Board not only appoints (and dismisses) the
        members of PostNL’s Board of Management (which is directly responsible for
        budgetary and business plan decisions), but also decides at its discretion which
        Board of Management decisions are subject to its approval (including the budget
        and business plan).

(28)    With a majority of votes representing 27.73% of the issued capital, VESA can
        determine the appointment (or reappointments) of the members of the Supervisory
        Board by agreeing to or vetoing the Supervisory Board’s proposals in this respect,
        granting VESA the ability to gradually replace all members of the Supervisory
        Board. With its majority, VESA can also determine the Shareholders’ Meeting
        position on the proposals of the Supervisory Board for remuneration policies of the
        members of the Supervisory Board and Board of Management, and can set the
        remuneration of each member of the Supervisory Board.

6.1.1.    The Commission’s previous practice

(54)      The Commission has previously considered mail delivery services (postal mail

          handle without specific equipment) and freight forwarding (which is the

6.2.      Relevant geographic markets for small package delivery services

6.2.1.    The Commission’s previous practice

(71)      In the past, the Commission has considered that the geographic scope of the
          markets for the provision of small package delivery services, and its subsegments,
          are likely national in scope. In particular, contracts for small package deliveries are
          mostly negotiated at national level for both domestic and international deliveries,
          and small package delivery companies need a significant national presence in a
          given country (e.g. infrastructure, client contracts, sales force) in order to
          meaningfully compete in that country. However, the Commission noted that there
          may be a tendency towards broader markets.106

6.2.2.    The Parties’ views

(72)      The Parties consider that, while certain Member States indeed function as national
          markets, certain groupings of other Member States may be considered as regional
          markets for small package delivery services. The Parties argue that Belgium,
          Luxembourg and the Netherlands function as one regional market given their
          shared geography (low and flat), similar economic size (proportionate to
          population) and similar culture (to the extent that the countries are colloquially
          grouped together as Benelux). These similarities allow service providers to expand
          easily, utilising similar pricing and marketing as in the Benelux Member State
          where they are headquartered. Therefore, the Parties submit that the geographic
          scope of the market should be defined as the Benelux.107

7.      COMPETITIVE ASSESSMENT

7.1.    Legal framework

(76)    Article 2 of the Merger Regulation requires the Commission to examine whether
        notified concentrations are compatible with the internal market, by assessing,
        pursuant to Articles 2(2) and (3), whether they would significantly impede
        effective competition in the internal market or in a substantial part of it, in
        particular as a result of the creation or strengthening of a dominant position.

(77)    Pursuant to Article 6(1)(b) of the Merger Regulation, where the Commission finds
        that the notified concentration does not raise serious doubts as to its compatibility
        with the internal market, the Commission shall decide not to oppose the
        concentration and declare it compatible with the internal market.

(78)    Horizontal effects are those deriving from a concentration where the undertakings
        concerned are actual or potential competitors of each other in one or more of the
        relevant markets concerned. The Commission appraises horizontal effects in
        accordance with the Horizontal Merger Guidelines.113 Horizontal effects may be
        non-coordinated or coordinated.

(79)    As regards horizontal non-coordinated effects, according to paragraph 26 of the
        Horizontal Merger Guidelines, a number of factors (the list of which is non
        exhaustive) may be taken into account in order to determine whether significant
        non-coordinated effects are likely to result from a concentration, including the
        combined entity’s market power, closeness of competition, the possibility for
        customers to switch suppliers, and barriers to entry and/or expand.

(80)    According to paragraph 27 of the Horizontal Merger Guidelines, the larger the
        market share, the more likely a firm is to possess market power. Furthermore, the
        larger the addition of market share, the more likely it is that a merger will lead to a
        significant increase in market power.

(81)    According to paragraphs 28 and ff. of the Horizontal Merger Guidelines, a merger
        between close competitors is more likely to have anticompetitive effects and lead
        to a significant increase in price. The higher the degree of substitutability between
        the merging firms’ products, the more likely it is that the merging firms will raise
        prices significantly.

(82)    According to paragraphs 31 and 32 of the Horizontal Merger Guidelines, another
        factor that may lead to a finding that significant non-coordinated effects are likely
        to result from a merger is whether customers have limited possibilities of switching
        suppliers or whether competitors are unlikely to increase their supply if prices
        increase post-merger.
