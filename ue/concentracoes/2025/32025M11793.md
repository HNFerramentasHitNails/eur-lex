5.1.1.    Product market definition

5.1.1.1. The Commission’s past practice
(15)      The Commission has recently considered the market for online retail supply of
          luxury goods in 2023 in case M.10926 Farfetch / Richemont / YNAP. In that case,
          the relevant product market was considered to be the online retail supply of multi-

5.1.1.3.1.      Luxury goods vs. mass-market goods
(19)     Luxury goods have been consistently distinguished from mass-market goods
         given that they are characterised by relatively high prices, rich creative content
         and are marketed under a prestige trademark. However, in its past practice, the
         Commission has recognised the difficulty in setting the boundaries of what
         constitutes “luxury”. (27)
(20)     The market investigation results confirm the distinction between luxury goods
         and mass-market goods, with the majority of respondents agreeing that these
         markets should be separated. (28) The Commission’s market investigation did not
         provide any element suggesting that the Commission should depart from its past
         decisional practice with respect to the distinction between luxury and mass-
         market goods.
(21)     Therefore, the Commission considers that luxury goods form a separate market
         from mass-market goods and will consequently conduct its competitive
         assessment on a separate retail market for luxury goods (excluding mass-market
         goods).

         substitutable, and (iii) consumers use online and offline for different purposes. (29)

5.1.1.3.4.      Multi-brand vs. mono-brand retailers
(28)     The Commission has in the past concluded, on a conservative basis, that multi-
         brand and mono-brand (i.e., brands’ DTC) channels form distinct markets. This
         is because, e.g., brands view multi-brand retailers as additional distribution
         channels rather than direct competitors and multi-brand retailers often consider
         that DTC channels are not close substitutes to their websites. (39)
(29)     The Commission’s market investigation broadly confirms this view. (40) Whilst
         the majority of market investigation respondents found that these channels
         compete at least to some extent, the responses were inconclusive in whether they
         compete closely. (41) According to the majority of brands providing views,
         consumers consider multi-product and DTC online channels as substitutes but not
         as close substitutes. (42)
(30)     Further, the majority of respondents found that the prices on both channels are
         broadly the same, but that they cater to somewhat different customers. (43) As
         explained by one brand, the substitutability between mono-brand and multi-brand
         websites “depends on whether [the consumers] know already what they want
         (speficic [sic] product / brand) - in which case they would probably go directly to
         the website of a specific brand, or whether they would prefer to browse and
         compare the offering of multiple brands”. (44)

6.        COMPETITIVE ASSESSMENT

6.1.      Legal framework
(68)      Under Article 2(2) and (3) of the Merger Regulation, the Commission must assess
          whether a proposed concentration would significantly impede effective
          competition in the internal market or in a substantial part of it, in particular
          through the creation or strengthening of a dominant position. In this respect, a
          merger can entail horizontal (non-coordinated and/or coordinated) and/or non-
          horizontal effects.
(69)      Horizontal effects are those deriving from a concentration where the undertakings
          concerned are actual or potential competitors of each other in one or more of the
          relevant markets concerned. The Commission assesses horizontal effects in
          accordance with the Horizontal Merger Guidelines. (87) According to
          paragraph 26 of the Horizontal Merger Guidelines, a non-exhaustive list of
          relevant factors needs to be assessed in order to determine whether significant
          non-coordinated effects are likely to result from a concentration. Accordingly,
          Sections 6.2.1 to 6.2.7 assess market shares, closeness of competition between the
          Parties, the alternatives to the Parties, possibility for brands and consumers to
          switch, entry and expansion barriers as well as out-of-market constraints. Based
          on all these factors considered together, conclusions on horizontal non-
          coordinated effects of the Transaction are drawn in Section 6.2.8.
(70)      Non-horizontal effects relate to the foreclosure of competitors that can arise from
          vertical integration. In general, vertical integration is the consequence of the
          combination of products, services or businesses across different levels of the same
          supply chain. Pursuant to the Non-Horizontal Merger Guidelines (88), vertical

6.2.4.    Luxury brands hold significant bargaining power

6.2.4.1. The Notifying Party’s view
(121)     According to the Notifying Party, brands play a key role in the personal luxury
          goods industry, as they both create and satisfy the consumer demand for personal
          luxury goods. (148) Thereby, brands are found to be powerful and influential
          players in the industry that are able to exert considerable competitive pressure on
          retailers. (149)
(122)     The Notifying Party explains that luxury brands use a mix of distribution channels
          and are increasingly able to develop strong DTC offerings to serve customers
          online. (150) The Notifying Party explains that focus on the DTC channel is
          considered desirable because it: (i) allows brands to capture the full proceeds of
          sales of their products, (ii) gives brands full control over their online presence,
          distribution and the supply chain allowing the brand to control the “luxury”
          experience, and (iii) allows brands to build relationships with consumers. (151)
          Therefore, some brands such as Chanel, Delvaux, Fendi, Hermès and Louis
          Vuitton have decided to only sell through DTC channels, and others are
          increasingly prioritising their DTC sales by, for example, limiting access to
          certain best-selling items to their own DTC channels. (152)
(123)     In the Notifying Party’s view, the Parties are not “must-have” distribution partners
          for luxury brands considering that most brands are present across multiple
          distribution channels (including their own DTC channels). Brands also have the
          ability to switch away from the Parties, should the Parties try to deteriorate any of
          the terms offered to their consumers. (153) The Notifying Party explains that
          brands are not contractually prevented from using alternative multi-brand online
          retailers as the Parties do not generally have exclusive agreements preventing the
          brands they are supplying from expanding (or changing) distribution to other
          retailers. (154) In view of the above, brands’ revenues from any given multi-brand
          online retailer are considered to be relatively small allowing them to negotiate
          from a position of relative strength. (155) Therefore, brands have the possibility to
          leverage their power vis-à-vis the Parties and other multi-brand retailers which,
          according to the Notifying Party, will not change as a result of the
          Transaction. (156)

6.2.6.    Barriers to entry and expansion

6.2.6.1. The Notifying Party’s view
(135)     The Notifying Party submits that the retail luxury goods market (both at the EEA-
          level and nationally) is dynamic and generally characterised by low barriers to
          expansion and entry for all types of retailers. According to the Notifying Party,
          the main prerequisite for successful expansion/entry in the retail supply of luxury
          goods is establishing relationships with the luxury brands (172) as well as having
          a sophisticated e-commerce environment (173) and the necessary staff (i.e.,
          sales/buying team, marketing team and customer services team). (174)
(136)     In terms of logistics, the Notifying Party explains that online retailers can
          externalise their logistics to shipping and delivery companies such as DHL and
          FedEx, and that international delivery companies can bridge the capability gap

          exchange rate handling. Similarly, access to warehouses and fulfilment centres

6.2.7.    Relevance of the out-of-market constraint

6.2.7.1. The Notifying Party’s view
(151)     The Notifying Party submits that the luxury retail space is fragmented and all sales
          channels of luxury goods, including online and offline and multi- and mono-brand
          channels, compete for the same consumers and exert strong competitive
          constraints on each other. (201) Therefore, should brands’ DTC channels, offline
          channels and mono-product channels not be considered to form part of the same
          market with online multi-brand, multi-product retail channels of luxury goods,

          Commission's decision in M.10926 Farfetch/Richemont/YNAP. (202)

                 sellers’ online and offline channels. (203)
          (b)    DTC (mono-brand) channels: Consumers perceive the DTC channel as a
                 direct substitute to the multi-brand channel. Also, brands rely
                 interchangeably on a range of channels including DTC and multi-brand
                 retailers. (204)

6.2.7.2. The Commission’s assessment
(153)    As explained in Section 5.1.1.3, for the purposes of this decision, the Commission
         has considered the narrowest plausible relevant product market to be that of online
         retail supply of multi-brand, multi-product personal luxury goods (excl. pre-
         owned goods). Under this conservative approach, the personal luxury goods sales
         made via other channels, such as luxury brands’ websites and brick-and-mortal
         outlets, or supply of pre-owned luxury goods, are not considered part of the same
         market.
(154)    However, the market investigation has confirmed that there are a wide range of
         options beyond online multi-brand, multi-product retailers that allow consumers
         to purchase the same luxury goods at similar prices and that will remain available
         post-Transaction. (207) These out-of-market constraints play an important role in
         the luxury goods retail space, as the Commission has found in its past
         practice. (208)
(155)    Indeed, the Commission has previously considered that, based on market size data
         from industry reports, only about 19% of total sales of personal luxury goods in
         the EEA in 2022 could be attributed to the online sales channel, with the
         remaining 81% of sales made via the offline channel. Further, of those 19% of
         total sales of personal luxury goods that are sold online, only about 53% can be
         attributed to the multi-brand, multi-product segment (i.e., only 10% of the total
         sales). (209) This means that most of the personal luxury goods purchased by
         consumers overall are purchased via distribution channels that are not part of the
         same market as the Parties. However, when making their strategic decisions (e.g.,
         to increase prices or otherwise worsen terms), the Parties must take into account
         the potential diversion of their sales to these out-of-market distribution channels.
(156)    The out-of-market constraints raised by the Notifying Party, i.e., (i) offline
         channels, (ii) DTC channels, (iii) mono-product retailers and (iv) pre-owned
         goods, are considered in turn below.

          retains a distinct appeal tied to status and innovation.” (245)

6.2.7.3. Conclusion on the relevance of out-of-market constraints
(168)     In light of the above evidence as well as the lack of barriers to switching between
          retailers (see Section 6.2.5), the Commission considers that, for the purposes of
          this decision, out-of-market constraints from the above-discussed sales channels
          and products currently exert and will continue post-Transaction to exert a certain
          level of competitive constraint on the Parties and other players active in the online
          retail of new multi-brand, multi-product personal luxury goods.

6.2.8.2.1.      Observations on the likely impact of the Transaction on business customers
(177)    Based on the evidence reviewed, the Commission considers that the Transaction

6.3.      Vertical effects
(184)     In addition to the above-described horizontal overlap, the Transaction gives rise
          to two vertical relationships, between:
          (a)    YNAP’s upstream activities in the manufacturing of luxury goods and
                 Mytheresa’s downstream activities as an (online) retailer of personal luxury
                 goods; and
          (b)    YNAP’s upstream activities on the market for the supply of e-commerce
                 services and Mytheresa’s downstream activities as an online
                 retailer/operator of an e-commerce platform.

6.3.1.    Manufacturing of personal luxury goods – YNAP (upstream) / Online retail
          supply of luxury goods – Mytheresa (downstream)
(185)     YNAP is a manufacturer of a small set of its own brands (42 Suns, 8 by Yoox,
          Iris & Ink, Mr P., Mr Porter Grooming, Net-a-Porter, Net-a-Porter for The King’s
          Foundation, Porte & Paire, and Yoox Net-a-Porter for The Prince’s
          Foundation.). (276) As described above, Mytheresa is active as a retailer of
          personal luxury goods. Therefore, the Transaction gives rise to a vertical
          relationship between YNAP as a manufacturer and wholesale supplier of personal
          luxury goods and Mytheresa as a retailer of personal luxury goods.
(186)     The Notifying Party submits that, irrespective of the approach taken for the
          definition of the downstream retail market, vertical concerns can be clearly
          excluded, as YNAP’s activities in this area are de minimis (EUR […] of YNAP’s
          GMV is generated with respect to the distribution of its own labels), and already
          pre-Transaction its own labels are exclusively distributed via YNAP stores. (277)
