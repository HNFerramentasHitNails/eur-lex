4.1.2.    The Notifying Parties’ views
(15)      The Notifying Parties submit that rental of retail spaces in OCs forms part of a
          wider segment for the rental of retail spaces, encompassing on-street retail units,
          retail parks and shopping centres (including shopping centres of at least 8,000
          sqm and 10,000 sqm previously distinguished by the Commission). This is
          because retailers are active across all types of retail centres and seek locations
          based on access to consumers and the shopping environment rather than the type
          of service provided. In addition, the Notifying Parties consider that OCs and
          shopping centres of a similar size offer a comparable shopping experience to
          consumers, such as (i) similar amenities (car parks, leisure and food/beverage
          facilities); (ii) a selection of premium/luxury and mid-range brands; (iii)
          convenient location (even if most of OCs are located out of town but they are
          easily accessible from the city centre); (iv) sales and promotions offered
          throughout the year. Ultimately, the Notifying Parties submit that the exact
          product market definition can be left open, since the Transaction does not raise
          competition concerns under any plausible market definition.

4.1.3.    The Commission’s assessment
(16)      The market investigation did not provide clear indications that rental of retail
          spaces in OCs should be treated as a distinct market segment from the wider
          market segment for the rental of retail spaces. Several customers (i.e. retailers)

          however, held a contrary view and considered that OCs are regular shopping
          centres and compete with regular real estate. One customer indicated that they
          consider all new retail opportunities in all forms of real estate, while another
          suggested that they would not open a mainline store next to an OC store for fear
          of cannibalisation. Overall, it would appear that the approach might be dependent
          on a retailer’s commercial strategy and product and pricing mix.

5.2.    Legal framework
(25)    Under Article 2(2) and (3) of the Merger Regulation, the Commission must assess
        whether a proposed concentration would significantly impede effective
        competition in the internal market or in a substantial part of it, in particular
        through the creation or strengthening of a dominant position. In this respect, a
        merger may entail horizontal and/or non-horizontal effects.
(26)    As regards the assessment of horizontal overlaps (meaning when the undertakings
        concerned are active in the same relevant markets), the Horizontal Merger
        Guidelines distinguish between two main ways in which mergers between actual
        or potential competitors may significantly impede effective competition, namely
        non-coordinated and coordinated effects. (19)

(27)    A merger giving rise to horizontal non-coordinated effects might significantly
        impede effective competition by creating or strengthening the dominant position
        of a single firm, one which, typically, would have an appreciably larger market
        share than the next competitor post-merger. Moreover, also mergers that do not
        lead to the creation of or the strengthening of a single firm’s dominant position
        may give rise to competition concerns under the substantive test set out in Article
        2(2) and Article 2(3) of the Merger Regulation. Regarding mergers in
        oligopolistic markets, the Merger Regulation clarifies that “under certain
        circumstances, concentrations involving the elimination of important competitive
        constraints that the merging parties exerted upon each other, as well as a
        reduction of competitive pressure on the remaining competitors, may, even in the
        absence of a likelihood of coordination between the members of the oligopoly,
        result in a significant impediment to effective competition”. (20)
(28)    The Horizontal Merger Guidelines list a number of factors which may influence
        whether or not non-coordinated effects are likely to result from a merger, such as
        large market shares of the merging firms, the fact that the merging firms are close
        competitors, the limited possibilities for customers to switch suppliers, the fact
        that the merger would eliminate an important competitive force, or the existence
        of barriers to entry. (21) Not all those factors need to be present to make significant
        non-coordinated effects likely and it is not an exhaustive list. (22)
