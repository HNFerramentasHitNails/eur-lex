4.2.2.2. Notifying Parties’ views
(24)    The Notifying Parties submit that there is no need to segment the market for the
        wholesale supply and acquisition of TV channels. (20)
(25)    The Notifying Parties note that a segmentation between FTA channels and pay-
        TV channels would not be appropriate as customers expect to be provided with a
        broad line-up of channels in the retail pay-TV offering they subscribe to, and
        therefore retail TV providers aim to acquire many channels from a range of
        channel suppliers for incorporation into their retail TV offerings. (21) In addition,
        the same content can be available both on FTA and on pay-TV channels. (22)
(26)    Further, the Notifying Parties note that a segmentation between basic pay-TV and
        premium pay-TV channels would not be appropriate, because basic and premium
        pay-TV channels compete with each other for content as well as viewers' time,
        attention and spending. They are largely interchangeable from the demand-side
        perspective, as many households switch from one to another when making content
        choices. Also, the target audience is generally the same for basic and premium
        pay-TV channels. (23)
(27)    As regards the segmentation by genre or thematic content, the Notifying Parties
        state that, from a demand-side perspective, consumers’ choice of a retail AV
        package is not due to one particular genre. From a supply-side perspective, retail
        AV providers would aim to offer a complementary range of genres, in order to
        meet the expectations of the viewers to be provided with content diversity. As a
        result, a segmentation by genre or thematic content would not be appropriate. (24)
(28)    As to the segmentation between linear and non-linear services, the Notifying
        Parties submit that it is irrelevant. Consumers would increasingly expect to be
        able to ‘interact’ with linear TV channels and benefit from built-in non-linear
        services (such as video on demand, pay per view, personal video recorder and
        catch-up services). Therefore, linear TV channels would increasingly offer
        ancillary non-linear services to enhance the viewers’ experience. As such, non-
        linear services would be ancillary to the main linear TV channels and licensed by
        the TV broadcasters to the TV distributors along with, or in addition to, those
        linear TV channels and not on a distinct standalone basis separately from the TV
        channels to which they relate. (25)
(29)    Last, the Notifying Parties submit that the competitive conditions in the market
        for the wholesale supply and acquisition of TV channels are the same irrespective
        of the distribution technology and the type of infrastructure used for the
        distribution of the TV channels to the viewers. In addition, all means of
        distribution used for the provision of AV services (DTT, DTH, or via the internet)
        would be available to the vast majority of viewers. Therefore, a segmentation of

4.3.2.2. Notifying Parties’ views
(42)    The Notifying Parties submit that within the overall market for the retail supply
        of AV services different segments should be identified for FTA and pay-TV
        services in view of the following differences in business models for FTA and pay-
        TV services: (i) a specific license is required to provide FTA channels of
        panhellenic coverage, and the number of such licenses is limited, whereas such
        limitations as to the number of licenses do not exist for pay-TV offerings; (ii) FTA
        TV channels with general information programme of panhellenic coverage have
        obligations relating to their programming scheme which do not apply to pay-TV
        channels; (iii) FTA channels are available to viewers free of charge, whereas pay-
        TV offerings are only available to viewers paying a subscription fee; (iv) FTA
        channels finance their operations via advertising revenues, while pay-TV retailers
        generate revenues through subscription fees; (v) unlike FTA TV services, pay-TV
        services are distributed in encrypted format and require a set-top box to receive
        them. (41)
(43)    Further, the Notifying Parties submit that a segmentation between basic pay-TV
        and premium pay-TV services would not be appropriate, as the boundaries
        between what constitutes premium and non-premium content are not clear. The
        success of any specific content would not be dependent on the production budget
        and cannot be predicted when it is produced. The ‘premium’ nature of content
        would be ephemeral – the commercial value of a particular piece of content would
        change over time. Moreover, producers would not specialise in either premium or
        basic content. (42)
(44)    As to the segmentation between lineal and non-linear services, the Notifying
        Parties submit that it is irrelevant. Linear and non-linear services would have
        converged to the extent that it has become increasingly difficult to distinguish
        between them. Consumers would view linear and non-linear AV services as fully
        substitutable and pay-TV providers would commonly package linear and non-
        linear services within a single subscription. Further, much of the content made

(62)    In this respect, a merger may entail horizontal and/or non-horizontal (namely,
        vertical or conglomerate) effects. Horizontal effects are those deriving from a
        concentration where the undertakings concerned are actual or potential
        competitors of each other in one or more of the relevant markets concerned.
        Vertical effects are those deriving from a concentration where the undertakings
        concerned are active on different or multiple levels of the supply chain.

        undertakings concerned are in a relationship which is neither horizontal nor
        vertical. A concentration may involve all three types of effects. In such a case, the
        Commission will appraise horizontal and non-horizontal effects in accordance
        with the guidance set out in the relevant notices, that is to say the Horizontal
        Merger Guidelines and the Non-Horizontal Merger Guidelines.
(63)    In assessing the competitive effects of a merger, the Commission compares the
        competitive conditions that would result from the notified merger with the
        conditions that would have prevailed without the merger. In most cases, the
        competitive conditions existing at the time of the merger constitute the relevant
        comparison for evaluating the effects of a merger. However, in some
        circumstances, the Commission may take into account future changes to the
        market that can reasonably be predicted. (62)
(64)    In relation to horizontal effects, the Horizontal Merger Guidelines (63) describe
        two main ways in which horizontal mergers may significantly impede effective
        competition. In particular, the proposed concentration might be creating or
        strengthening a dominant position: (i) by eliminating important competitive
        constraints on one or more firms, which consequently would have increased
        market power, without resorting to coordinated behaviour (non-coordinated
        effects); and (ii) by changing the nature of competition in such a way that firms
        that previously were not coordinating their behaviour, are significantly more
        likely to coordinate and raise prices or otherwise harm effective competition
        (coordinated effects).
(65)    A merger giving rise to horizontal non-coordinated effects might significantly
        impede effective competition by creating or strengthening the dominant position
        of a single firm, one which, typically, would have an appreciably larger market
        share than the next competitor post-merger. Moreover, also mergers that do not
        lead to the creation of or the strengthening of a single firm’s dominant position
        may create competition concerns under the substantive test set out in Article 2(2)
        and Article 2(3) of the Merger Regulation. Regarding mergers in oligopolistic
        markets, the Merger Regulation clarifies that ‘under certain circumstances,
        concentrations involving the elimination of important competitive constraints that
        the merging parties exerted upon each other, as well as a reduction of competitive
        pressure on the remaining competitors, may, even in the absence of a likelihood
        of coordination between the members of the oligopoly, result in a significant
        impediment to effective competition’. (64)

          firm has such a significant degree of market power, can it be expected that it will
          significantly influence the conditions of competition in the upstream market and
          thus, possibly, the prices and supply conditions in the downstream market. (74)

          the ability to substantially foreclose access to inputs, second, whether it would

          a significant detrimental effect on competition downstream. (75)
