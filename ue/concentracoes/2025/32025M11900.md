4.1.1.    Product market definition

4.1.1.1. Market for the manufacture and sale of surgical equipment (upstream)
(7)       There are two broad types of surgery:
          (a) Open surgery, which refers to surgical operations where the surgeon
              performs a large incision to access the patient’s relevant organ directly.
          (b) “Keyhole” surgery, which refers to minimally invasive surgery via the
              insertion of surgical tools through one or more small incisions or through
              natural body orifices. While this type of surgery may not be available in every
              situation, such minimally invasive processes generally have shorter recovery
              times, better aesthetic outcome, and lower risks of infection than open
              surgery. There are several types of keyhole surgeries including:
                o Laparoscopy, which entails the insertion of surgical tools and a camera
                  through several small incisions to allow the surgeon to see the inside of
                  the relevant organ and perform the surgery. In this case, the surgeon
                  directly (i.e., by hand) manipulates the surgical tools to conduct surgery
                  the patient on the operating table.
                o Robotically-assisted surgery (“RAS”), which also involves small
                  incisions, a camera and surgical instruments. However, the instruments
                  are operated remotely by the surgeon via a console, and a robotic arm
                  manipulates said instruments to conduct surgery on the patient. RAS can
                  enable a surgeon to benefit from greater flexibility, stability and range
                  of motion than what may be possible for a surgeon operating equipment
                  by hand.
(8)       Equipment for these different types of surgery can vary significantly with open
          surgery requiring cutting and other handheld instruments for the surgeon (such as
          a scalpel, forceps, scissors, clamps, etc), laparoscopy requiring smaller tools in
          addition to a camera equipped catheter, and RAS requiring a robotic arm and all
          its associated tips.

4.1.1.1.1.      The Commission’s past practice
(9)       The Commission has not previously assessed the manufacture and sale of surgical
          equipment specifically.
(10)      Nevertheless, in Johnson & Johnson/Guidant, the Commission established that in
          the context of cardiac surgery devices, keyhole surgery equipment (in that case
          endoscopic vessel harvesting systems) was on a different market than traditional
          heart surgery equipment. (6) The Commission further detailed that, although both
          types of surgery delivered the same results, they differed “considerably in terms
          of procedure, required surgical skills, patient’s recovery time and risk of
          complications.” In addition, the Commission highlighted that the price of
          equipment for keyhole surgery was considerably higher than that for traditional
          open surgery.

4.1.1.1.2.      The Notifying Party’s views
(12)     The Notifying Party submits that the surgical tools used for open surgery and
         those used for keyhole surgery are on the same market.
(13)     This is mostly because the surgical techniques, i.e., open surgery and keyhole
         surgery (whether laparoscopy or RAS), may be and are regularly used
         interchangeably for the same types of surgical interventions. The choice between
         surgical techniques is made according to the patient’s characteristics and the
         surgeon’s preferences.
(14)     On the demand-side, the Notifying Party submits that the costs of the different
         procedures are comparable considering the different financing options of the
         equipment such as leasing, which make RAS equipment, for example, just as
         affordable as equipment for open surgery. A potential difference in costs is also
         particularly irrelevant in the Territories considering that the reimbursement
         systems in place tend to not differentiate between the types of surgery and can
         sometimes allow for higher reimbursement rates for equipment such as RAS
         equipment. In addition, since keyhole surgeries allow for shorter recovery times
         and reduced treatments, a potential difference in upfront cost between open and
         keyhole surgery would be negated when looking at the overall cost of the surgical
         procedure (including post-operation time and costs).
(15)     On the supply-side, the Notifying Party submits that there are many players active
         in the manufacture of RAS, laparoscopy, and open surgery equipment.
(16)     Finally, the Notifying Party submits that the regulatory landscape is the same for
         all types of surgical equipment namely the Medical Devices Regulation. (8)
(17)     With respect to a potential further segmentation of equipment for keyhole surgery
         based on the specific type of surgery (e.g. laparoscopy and RAS), the Notifying

         procedures, and it is the surgeon that chooses which technique to use. They further

4.1.2.    Geographic market definition

4.1.2.1. Market for the manufacture and sale of surgical equipment

4.1.2.1.1.      The Commission past practice
(44)      The Commission has not previously defined a geographic market for the
          manufacture and sale of surgical equipment used for open surgery, laparoscopy
          or RAS.
(45)      With respect to other medical devices, however, generally, the Commission has
          previously found that the markets were national for several reasons including the
          different reimbursement schemes from one country to the other, the procurement
          processes that are usually issued at national level (e.g. public tenders), the price
          variations between countries, and the presence of different market participants in
          each country. (37)

4.1.2.1.2.      The Notifying Party’s views
(46)      The Notifying Party submits that the market for the manufacture and sale of
          surgical equipment is at least EEA-wide. This is due to the facts that
          manufacturers and suppliers operate on a cross-border basis facilitated by a
          harmonised EU regulation. (38)
(47)      The Notifying Party further submits that customers make their purchases on the
          basis of supplier reputation and cost-effectiveness rather than based on the
          location of said supplier.

4.1.2.1.3.      The Commission’s assessment
(48)      The market investigation broadly confirmed the Commission’s past practice.
          Indeed, the majority of responding market participants submitted that competition
          for the sale of RAS equipment occurs primarily at national level. (39) Similarly,
          the majority of respondents submitted that pricing and contractual conditions for
          RAS equipment are determined country-by-country (40) as are the reimbursement

5.1.      Legal framework

5.1.1.    Non-horizontal mergers
(56)      A merger can entail non-horizontal effects when it involves companies operating
          at different levels of the same supply chain or in closely related markets. Pursuant
          to the Commission Guidelines on the assessment of non-horizontal mergers under

          the Council Regulation on the control of concentrations between undertakings
          (the “Non-Horizontal Merger Guidelines”), (46) non-horizontal mergers do not
          entail the loss of direct competition between merging firms in the same relevant
          market and provide scope for efficiencies. However, there are circumstances in
          which non-horizontal mergers may significantly impede effective competition.
          This is in particular the case if they give rise to foreclosure. (47)
(57)      Vertical mergers involve companies operating at different levels of the supply
          chain, for example, when a manufacturer of a certain product merges with one of
          its distributors.
(58)      In assessing potential vertical effects of a merger, the Commission analyses
          whether a merger results in foreclosure so that actual or potential rivals’ access to
          supplies or markets is hampered or eliminated as a result of the merger, thereby
          reducing these companies’ ability and/or incentive to compete. Such foreclosure
          may discourage the entry or expansion of rivals or encourage their exit.
          Foreclosure thus can be found even if the foreclosed rivals are not forced to exit
          the market: it is sufficient that the rivals are disadvantaged and consequently led
          to compete less effectively. Such foreclosure is regarded as anti-competitive
          where the merging companies—and, possibly, some of their competitors as
          well—are as a result able to profitably increase the price charged to consumers.
(59)      The Non-Horizontal Merger Guidelines distinguish between two forms of
          foreclosure. (48) The first is where the merger is likely to raise the costs of
          downstream rivals by restricting their access to an important input (input
          foreclosure). The second is where the merger is likely to foreclose upstream rivals
          by restricting their access to a sufficient customer base (customer foreclosure).
(60)      In assessing both types of foreclosure, the Commission applies the ability,
          incentive, effects framework. This implies the assessment of three cumulative
          conditions, i.e. whether (1) the merged entity would have the ability to engage in
          foreclosure, (2) it would have the incentive to do so, and (3) what would be the
          overall impact on effective competition in the affected markets.
(61)      Finally, as acknowledged in the Non-Horizontal Guidelines, the merged entity
          may gain access to commercially sensitive information regarding the upstream or
          downstream activities of rivals. For instance, by becoming the supplier of a
          downstream competitor, a company may obtain critical information, which allows
          it to price less aggressively in the downstream market to the detriment of
          consumers. Such acquisition of commercially sensitive information may also put
          competitors at a competitive disadvantage, thereby dissuading them from entering
          or expanding in the market. (49)

5.1.2.    Conglomerate effects

(62)      Conglomerate mergers are mergers between firms that are active in closely related
          markets (e.g. mergers involving suppliers of complementary products or of
          products which belong to a range of products that is generally purchased by the
          same set of customers for the same end use). These firms are in a relationship
          which is neither purely horizontal (as competitors in the same relevant market)
          nor vertical (as supplier and customer). According to the Non-Horizontal

(63)    However, foreclosure effects may arise when the combination of products in
        related markets may confer on the merged entity the ability and incentive to
        leverage a strong market position from one market to another closely related
        market by means of tying or bundling or other exclusionary practices. While tying
        and bundling have often no anticompetitive consequences, in certain

        competitors' ability or incentive to compete. This may reduce the competitive
        pressure on the merged entity allowing it to increase prices. (51)

(64)    In assessing the likelihood of such a scenario, the Commission examines, first,
        whether the merged firm would have the ability to foreclose its rivals (52), second,
        whether it would have the economic incentive to do so (53) and, third, whether a
        foreclosure strategy would have a significant detrimental effect on competition,
        thus causing harm to consumers. (54) In practice, these factors are often examined
        together as they are closely intertwined.

(65)    In order to be able to foreclose competitors, the merged entity must have a
        significant degree of market power, which does not necessarily amount to
        dominance, in one of the markets concerned. The effects of bundling or tying can
        only be expected to be substantial when at least one of the merging parties’
        products is viewed by many customers as particularly important and there are few
        relevant alternatives for that product. (55) Further, for foreclosure to be a potential
        concern, it must be the case that there is a large common pool of customers, which
        is more likely to be the case when the products are complementary. (56) Finally,
        bundling is less likely to lead to foreclosure if rival firms are able to deploy
        effective and timely counter-strategies, such as single-product companies
        combining their offers. (57)

(66)    The incentive to foreclose rivals through bundling or tying depends on the degree
        to which this strategy is profitable. (58) Bundling and tying may entail losses or
        foregone revenues for the merged entity. (59) However, they may also allow the
        merged entity to increase profits by gaining market power in the tied goods
        market, protecting market power in the tying good market, or a combination of
        the two. (60)
(67)    It is only when a sufficiently large fraction of market output is affected by

5.4.2.    Notifying Party’s views
(95)      As regards the ability of the merged entity to engage in a conglomerate
          foreclosure strategy, the Notifying Party argues that the merged entity would have
          no ability to tie or bundle Intuitive RAS equipment with mixed products such as
          EasyKits. Hospitals as customers, both public and private, exercise significant
          buyer power: public hospitals define their requirements in tender documentation,
          while private hospitals select mixed contracts only after reviewing offers from
          multiple vendors. Also, the Target does not currently tie or bundle these products,
          with approximately […]% of Intuitive RAS sales in 2024 occurring without
          accompanying […]. Rival kit suppliers could also partner with surgical equipment
          manufacturers to offer complete solutions, mitigating any potential ability of the
          combined entity to foreclose competition. (87)
(96)      As regards the incentive of the merged entity to engage in a conglomerate
          foreclosure strategy, the Notifying Party argues that it has no incentive to
          foreclose rival kit suppliers through tying or bundling. Mixed contracts will
          continue only for their remaining duration (if hospitals so choose), and Intuitive
          does not intend to participate in future calls for tenders for mixed products. (88)
(97)      As regards the impact of a potential conglomerate foreclosure strategy, the
          Notifying Party argues that any attempt to foreclose rival suppliers would have
          no impact on effective competition. To the extent that the merged entity will
          inherit any mixed contracts, these are limited in duration. Other than this legacy
          business, the merged entity will not provide any of the mixed products, and so
          will not be able to tie or bundle any such products post-Transaction. As regards
          EasyKits specifically, customized kits are required for all surgical procedures,
          while Intuitive’s RAS is used only in a small minority. […]. Any foreclosure
          would also be temporary, limited to the duration of existing mixed contracts. (89)

(100)     Moreover, the market investigation indicates that, while hospitals may procure
          RAS systems together with mixed products (such as drapes, insufflation systems,
          patient positioning devices, surgical tables, and sterilisation baskets), respondents
          largely confirmed that, in practice, hospitals alone decide, based on their needs,
          whether to include RAS and other ancillary surgical equipment in the same tender
          or separately. One market participant noted that “Hospitals decide tender
          specifications and needs” while another stated that “the buyer has the option to
          choose whether to purchase everything from a single supplier or separate the
          supplies of individual devices”, and a third explained that “RAS equipment is too
          specific an item to be mixed in a tender with other equipment”. Another confirmed
          that “hospitals can determine the scope of the tender”. As a result, the merged
          entity is unlikely to be able to foreclose suppliers of mixed products through tying
          or bundling, as hospitals retain full discretion over the structure of their
          tenders. (92)
(101)     Second, the merged entity will not have the incentive to engage in a tying or
          bundling strategy with respect to the mixed products, as it will only supply
          existing customers until the abovementioned contract expiry and will not be active
          thereafter post-Transaction. It therefore would not be in a position to tie or bundle
          them, nor would it gain any benefit from attempting to do so.
(102)     Finally, with respect to the possible impact of the Transaction, consistent with the
          above, the majority of respondents to the Commission’s market investigation
          considered that the likely impact on mixed products would be neutral or positive
          in each of the EEA Territories. (93)

5.4.4.    Conclusion on conglomerate effects
(103)     In light of the foregoing, given that the merged entity would be unlikely to have
          the ability or incentive to engage in a tying or bundling strategy with respect to
          the mixed products and considering the limited effect such a strategy would have,
          the Commission concludes that the Transaction is unlikely to give rise to serious
          doubts as to its compatibility with the internal market and with the EEA
          Agreement as a result of any conglomerate effects.
