5.1.      Animal pharmaceuticals

5.1.1.    Product market definition

5.1.1.1. The Commission’s previous practice

(12)       In previous cases in this sector, the Commission has divided animal health
           products into three main categories: (i) biologicals (such as vaccines), (ii)
           pharmaceuticals, and (iii) feed supplements.9

(13)       In the present case, the Parties’ activities give rise to affected markets only with
           respect to marketed animal pharmaceuticals. Animal pharmaceuticals encompass a
           wide group of products that contain a variety of active substances to prevent or
           treat a large range of animal diseases and disorders. Within pharmaceuticals, the
           Commission has distinguished between pharmaceuticals across various therapeutic
           areas, in particular it has distinguished between parasiticides, otitis products,
           antimicrobials, endocrine treatments, anti-inflammatories, and analgesics.10

(14)       The Commission has also conducted separate assessments for food production
           animals (“PA”, i.e. swine, poultry, bovine) and companion animals (“CA”, mostly
           cats and dogs).

(15)       In addition, when defining relevant product markets in the area of animal
           pharmaceuticals, the Commission considered on a case-by-case basis the following
           factors as important as they might lead to separate markets:11 (i) species (e.g. cats
           and dogs within CA; poultry, swine and cattle within PA, equine), (ii) active
           ingredient/chemical class, (iii) targeted pathology/indication, (iv) mode of
           administration (“MoA”) (e.g. oral, topical, injection), (v) duration of efficacy for
           PA; and (vi) duration of the withdrawal period for PA12. Not each factor was
           considered for each type of pharmaceuticals.

5.1.1.2. The Notifying Party’s view

(16)       The Notifying Party agrees with the distinction between veterinary (i) biologicals,
           (ii) pharmaceuticals, and (iii) feed supplements.13 The Notifying Party further
           submits that, given that the Transaction gives rise to vertical links only, further
           segmentation of the market for animal pharmaceuticals is not necessary.14

5.1.1.3. The Commission’s assessment

(17)       The market investigation has confirmed the Commission’s decisional practice with
           respect to the product market definition of animal pharmaceuticals.

(18)       In particular, the vast majority of market participants responding to the market
           investigation agreed that: (i) animal pharmaceuticals form a product market
           distinct from animal biologicals and feed supplements, (ii) animal pharmaceuticals
           should be segmented by different animal groups (CA, PA and possibly equine),
           and that animal pharmaceuticals should be segmented by therapeutic areas.15

(19)       However, within a specific therapeutic area, various animal pharmaceuticals are
           not generally considered substitutable, as they are used for treatment of different
           indications. A veterinary pharmaceutical intended for a specific indication
           generally cannot be used for treatment of a different indication.16 For example, a
           Dechra product used for the treatment of Cushing’s disease would not be
           substitutable with a treatment for Addison’s disease even though both treatments
           belong to the field of endocrinology. Therefore, there is not always demand-side
           substitution for the various pharmaceuticals within a given therapeutic area.

(20)       In addition, market participants confirm that players active in an individual
           therapeutic area typically do not offer an entire portfolio of products in that area,17
           and thus the competitive landscape for different indications even within a
           therapeutic area can differ considerably. Supply-side substitution is thus not
           immediate between various pharmaceuticals belonging to a given therapeutic area.

(21)       In view of the above, for the purposes of this decision, the Commission considers
           it appropriate with respect to the assessment of vertical links between the Parties,
           to use the markets for animal pharmaceuticals across therapeutic areas as a starting
           point for the identification of affected markets but has based the more detailed
           assessment on the upstream markets for animal pharmaceuticals for each
           individual indication and/or for a specific animal group. Further differences
           between animal pharmaceuticals for a specific indication or animal group (which
           have an influence on the pool of available alternatives) based on the factors
           identified in paragraph (15) above, are taken into account in the Commission’s
           competitive assessment.

6.      COMPETITIVE ASSESSMENT

6.1.    Legal framework for competitive assessment of vertical mergers

(38)     Vertical mergers involve companies operating at different levels of the same
         supply chain. Pursuant to the Commission Guidelines on the assessment of non-
         horizontal mergers under the Council Regulation on the control of concentrations
         between undertakings (the “Non-Horizontal Merger Guidelines”)37, vertical
         mergers do not entail the loss of direct competition between merging firms in the
         same relevant market and provide scope for efficiencies.

(39)     However, there are circumstances in which vertical mergers may significantly
         impede effective competition. This is in particular the case if they give rise to
         foreclosure.38

(40)     The Non-Horizontal Merger Guidelines distinguish between two forms of
         foreclosure: input foreclosure, where the merger is likely to raise costs of
         downstream rivals by restricting their access to an important input, and customer
         foreclosure, where the merger is likely to foreclose upstream rivals by restricting
         their access to a sufficient customer base.39

(41)     Pursuant to the Non-Horizontal Merger Guidelines, input foreclosure arises where,
         post-merger, the new entity would be likely to restrict access to its actual or
         potential rival in the downstream market to the products or services that it would
         have otherwise supplied absent the merger, thereby raising its downstream rivals'
         costs by making it harder for them to obtain supplies of the input under similar
         prices and conditions as absent the merger.40

(42)     For input foreclosure to be a concern, the merged entity should have a significant
         degree of market power in the upstream market. Only when the merged entity has
         such a significant degree of market power, can it be expected that it will
         significantly influence the conditions of competition in the upstream market and
         thus, possibly, the prices and supply conditions in the downstream market.41

(43)     Pursuant to the Non-Horizontal Merger Guidelines, customer foreclosure may
         occur when a supplier integrates with an important customer in the downstream
         market and because of this downstream presence, the merged entity may foreclose
         access to a sufficient customer base to its actual or potential rivals in the upstream
         market (the input market) and reduce their ability or incentive to compete which in
         turn, may raise downstream rivals' costs by making it harder for them to obtain
         supplies of the input under similar prices and conditions as absent the merger. This

6.4.2.2. Incentive to foreclose

(152)     At the outset, as mentioned above in Section 6.1., the existence of customer
          foreclosure is based on the fulfilment of three cumulative conditions, i.e. (i) the
          ability to foreclose, (ii) the incentive to foreclose, and (iii) the impact of
          foreclosure. There will be no likely customer foreclosure where one of these
          conditions is missing. As concluded above, the Merged Entity will likely not have
          the ability to foreclose, hence excluding the likelihood of customer foreclosure.
          The Commission will nonetheless assess whether the two other conditions apply
          for sake of completeness.

(153)     The Commission notes that the large majority of the respondents (clinics and
          manufacturers) considered it realistic that post-Transaction IVCE would stop or
          significantly reduce purchasing and/or IVCE veterinarians would stop or reduce
          significantly prescribing pharmaceutical products that compete with Dechra’s
          pharmaceutical products. They notably referred to IVCE possibly gaining better
          pricing from Dechra.137

(154)     The incentive to engage in customer foreclosure will depend on the extent to
          which Dechra can benefit from possibly higher price levels in the upstream market
          arising because of Dechra’s rivals being foreclosed. In this case, it is unlikely that
          IVCE’s foreclosure attempt will harm competitors because IVCE’s purchasing
          share for pharmaceutical products is below 30% at Member State level (and most
          often below 10%),138 meaning that there are sufficient other alternatives on the
          market. Also, as mentioned above, IVCE generally does not account for a large
          part of the manufacturers’ revenues.
