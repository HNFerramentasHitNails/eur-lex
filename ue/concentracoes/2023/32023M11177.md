(9)     Generally, cancers can involve either “solid” or “liquid” tumours. Solid tumours

        distant parts of the body.11

        Commission takes account of the ESMO guidelines when analysing the product
        markets for oncology products.18

5.1.    Analytical framework

(16)    Article 2 of the Merger Regulation requires the Commission to examine whether
        notified concentrations are compatible with the internal market, by assessing
        whether they would significantly impede effective competition in the internal
        market or in a substantial part of it, in particular, as a result of the creation or
        strengthening of a dominant position or the removal of a significant competitive
        constraint.

(17)    In addition, Article 57(1) of the EEA Agreement requires the Commission to
        examine whether notified concentrations are compatible with the functioning of the
        EEA Agreement, by assessing whether they would create or strengthen a dominant
        position as a result of which effective competition would be significantly impeded
        within the EEA territory or a substantial part of it.

(18)    In this framework, “competition” is understood to mean product and price
        competition (actual or potential), as well as innovation competition, where the
        Commission assesses in particular potential horizontal non-coordinated effects.29
        The Commission considers that a concentration may not only affect competition in
        existing markets, but also competition in innovation and new product markets.30
        This may be the case when a concentration concerns entities currently developing
        new products or technologies which may one day replace existing ones or which
        are being developed for a new intended use and will therefore not replace existing
        products but create a completely new demand.31

(19)    In the pharmaceutical industry, the process of innovation is structured in such a
        way that it is typically possible to identify competing research programmes (or
        “pipeline” programmes) at an early stage of clinical trials. 32 Competing pipeline
        programmes can be defined as R&D efforts aimed at developing substitutable
        products and having similar timing. The level of progress of a research programme
        should be assessed by reference to the stage of the on-going preclinical or clinical
        trials.33

5.2.1.    Bladder cancer

(29)      Bladder cancer - also known as “urothelial carcinoma” (“UC”) – is a cancer that
          begins in the urothelial cells, which line the urethra, bladder, ureters, and renal
          pelvis.51

5.2.1.1. Product market definition

Commission’s decisional practice

(30)      The Commission has not previously considered in past decisions the relevant
          market for the treatment of bladder cancer and its potential segments.

The Notifying Party’s view

(31)      The Notifying Party argues that bladder cancer may be segmented by whether the
          cancer has invaded the muscle layer of the bladder wall and, if so, how far the
          cancer has spread. Bladder cancer that is restricted to the inner lining of the organ
          without having grown into the muscle wall is referred to as non-muscle invasive
          (“NMIBC”), and it is the earliest stage of the disease. Once the cancer has invaded
          the bladder muscle wall it is referred to as a muscle-invasive bladder cancer
          (“MIBC”); if it expands to lymph nodes or other parts of the body (such as the
          liver, bones, lungs etc.) it is referred to as locally advanced or metastatic bladder
          cancer (or metastatic urothelial carcinoma) (“mUC”).52

(32)      The type of treatment for bladder cancer depends on the cancer’s type, stage, and
          grade (i.e., how the cancer cells look like under the microscope and how much they
          look like normal cells). Broadly, for all types of UC, surgery and thus complete
          resection of all tumour tissue should be achieved, where possible, in the first
          instance. This may be accompanied by additional treatments given before or after
          the surgery.53

(33)      Patients with NMIBC are generally first treated through surgery with transurethral
          resection of bladder tumour. For patients with low-risk NMIBC, one single,
          immediate, intravesical chemotherapy installation would normally follow, in
          combination with continued cystoscopic surveillance. In patients with intermediate-
          risk NMIBC, additional courses of intravesical therapy are recommended to reduce
          risk of recurrence: this may consist of either further intravesical chemotherapy or,
          more often, intravesical instillation of Bacillus Calmette-Guerin (“BCG”), a live
          attenuated strain of Mycobacterium bovis bacteria - which represents the current
          standard of care.54 Patients who are not responsive to, are intolerant to, or relapse
          after BCG will then receive radical cystectomy if they are very high-risk patients;

Conclusion

(36)    In light of the above, the Commission takes the view that, a distinction should be
        made between NMIBC, MIBC, and mUC. In conclusion, in the present case, the
        exact market definition, i.e., whether the market shall be segmented by cancer

(68)      Cervical cancer develops in the cells of the cervix – the lower part of the uterus.

          begin to appear in the cervical tissue, they may create an environment for cancer to
          occur. Over time, if not destroyed or removed, the abnormal cells may become
          cancer cells that grow and spread more deeply into the cervix and surrounding

5.2.3.1. Product market definition

The Commission’s decisional practice

(69)      The Commission has not previously considered in past decisions the relevant
          market for the treatment of cervical cancer and its potential segments.

The Notifying Party’s view

(70)      The Notifying Party submits that cervical cancers should be classified into stages
          depending on the anatomic extent of the disease to give insight on appropriate
          treatment types and the likelihood of survival. More specifically, cervical cancers
          can be grouped into three stages depending on the extent of the disease: (i) early-
          stage cervical cancer (stages IA-IIA); (ii) locally advanced cervical cancer (stages
          IIB-IVA); and (iii) metastatic cervical cancer (stage IVB). Treatment options
          depend on the cervical cancer stage. In early-stage cervical cancer, surgery or
          radiation therapy can be used. A combination of chemotherapy and radiation
          therapy is used, e.g., for larger tumours. If the surgery is performed, radiation
          therapy with or without chemotherapy can mitigate the chances of cancer
          recurrence or spread (so-called adjuvant radiotherapy and chemotherapy
          respectively). For locally-advanced cervical cancer, the preferred therapy is the
          concurrent utilisation of chemoradiation rather than primary surgery, and
          neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by surgery or radiation therapy alone. For
          metastatic or recurrent cancer, paclitaxel and platinum chemotherapy in
          combination with pembrolizumab and bevacizumab are used as a first-line
          treatment based on the balance between efficacy, patient eligibility, and toxicity
          profile. Patients who progress after first-line therapy for recurrent or metastatic
          cervical cancer receive therapy with single-agent chemotherapy or anti-PD-1 if not
          previously used.106

(71)      The Notifying Party argues that, given the absence of competitive interaction

          their products on the market and in development as regards type of treatment,

5.2.6.    Lymphoma

(137)     The lymphatic system branches through all parts of the body, consisting of a large
          network of lymphatic vessels, lymph nodes, lymphoid organs, and lymphoid
          tissues. It carries a colourless liquid called lymph. This liquid circulates around the
          body tissues and contains a high number of white blood cells (lymphocytes) which
          can detect pathogens and fight infections.

(138)     In persons with lymphoma, some of the white blood cells (lymphocytes) do not
          work properly. They grow out of control, start to divide constantly, but never
          develop fully and cannot fight infections as normal white blood cells do. There are
          two main types of lymphocytes: B cells and T cells, and different types of
          lymphomas affect either B cells or T cells. The Notifying Party explains that
          lymphomas can be grouped into Hodgkin lymphomas (“HL”) and non-Hodgkin
          lymphomas (“NHL”), depending on the lymphoma cells’ characteristics. HL
          contains abnormal cells called Reed–Sternberg cells, named from the two
          pathologists that first described them. NHL is any type of lymphoma that is not
          HL. 178

(207)    In this respect, a merger may entail horizontal and/or non-horizontal (i.e. vertical or
         conglomerate) effects. Horizontal effects are those deriving from a concentration
         where the undertakings concerned are actual or potential competitors of each other
         in one or more of the relevant markets concerned. Non-horizontal effects are those
         deriving from a concentration where the undertakings concerned are active in
         different relevant markets.255

(208)    In the majority of circumstances, conglomerate mergers do not lead to any
         competition problems but in certain specific cases there may be harm to
         competition.256 The main concern in the context of conglomerate effects is that of
         foreclosure.257 Conglomerate mergers may allow the merged entity to combine
         products in related markets and this may confer on the merged entity the ability and
         incentive to leverage a strong market position from one market to another by means
         of tying or bundling, or other exclusionary practices.258

(209)    In assessing the likelihood of conglomerate effects, the Commission examines,
         first, whether the merged firm would have the ability to foreclose its rivals, second,
         whether it would have the economic incentive to do so and, third, whether a
         foreclosure strategy would have a significant detrimental effect on competition,
         thus causing harm to consumers. In practice, these factors are often examined
         together as they are closely intertwined.259

         Ability to foreclose

(213)    The Notifying Party submits that neither Pfizer nor Seagen generally uses a
         bundling strategy for its oncology products in the EEA (or otherwise conditions
         access or preferred pricing for oncology products in the EEA) in contracts with
         customers today. Pfizer has no plans to change this approach for either its own
         oncology products or those it would acquire in the Transaction. In particular, the
         Notifying Party notes that Pfizer’s and Seagen’s products are purchased and
         reimbursed separately, such that there are logistical and economic barriers to
         bundling them. For example, Seagen’s TUKYSA is a branded oral product and is
         primarily procured by customers through pharmacies, whereas Pfizer’s Trazimera
         is an intravenous drug that only healthcare providers procure and administer and
         would therefore not be dispensed by a pharmacy.261

(214)    In addition, Pfizer’s Trazimera, Zirabev, and Ruxience are biosimilars. Pfizer’s
         contracts for branded and biosimilar drugs are separate, as biosimilars are procured
         separately from branded products, typically via competitive tenders for the
         molecule. The purpose of such tenders is to obtain the best price for that particular
         molecule from the originator or from suppliers of the biosimilar versions. Providers
         would therefore have little incentive to agree to a bundle of any of Pfizer’s
         products with Seagen’s because they would be giving up their leverage in
         biosimilar tenders, which are typically organised by molecule, pitting different
         biosimilars against each other to compete largely on price.262

         Incentive to foreclose

(215)    The Notifying Party submits that Seagen’s products face strong competitors and
         attempts to condition those sales on sales of Pfizer’s biosimilars would result in a
         loss of sales. Such a strategy would not be profitable as a sale of Seagen’s branded
         products would be more profitable for Pfizer than a sale of its own biosimilar
         products.263

         Effects

(216)    As post-Transaction Pfizer would have no ability or incentive to engage in
         bundling or leveraging strategies to foreclose competitors, there are no adverse
         effects on competition.

(217)    The results of market investigation and discussions with KOLs largely confirmed
         the Notifying Party’ submissions. The Commission considers that the Transaction
         does not give rise to concerns with respect to any possible conglomerate
         relationships for the following reasons:

(218)    First, Pfizer will have no ability to foreclose following the Transaction. . The
         market investigation confirmed that biosimilars are procured separately from
         branded products, typically via competitive tenders for the molecule.264 KOLs
         confirmed that one of the main factors for purchasing oncology drugs, in particular
         biosimilars, is price.265 A KOL explained that the choice of a specific biosimilar is
         made by hospitals more broadly, rather than individual oncologists. Therefore,
         “when a pipeline drug is approved as a biosimilar of another compound, the
         scientific community assumes that they are equal in terms of efficacy and safety
         therefore from medical oncologists’ perspective there is no substantial difference
         between approved drugs irrespective of the company that produce the biosimilar”
         and, consequently, “hospitals usually choose the ideal biosimilar depending on a
         cost-efficiency basis […].”266 Zirabev, for example is a biosimilar form of
         bevacizumab and Trazimera a biosimilar of trastuzumab. As confirmed by the
         market investigation results, there are many competing biosimilar forms of both
         bevacizumab and trastuzumab widely available in the market.267 It would therefore
         not be profitable to bundle Seagen’s branded products with Pfizer’s biosimilars, as
         customers would want to remain free to purchase the cheapest biosimilar available
         at any given time.

(219)    Second, Pfizer will have no incentive to foreclose following the Transaction. The
         results of the market investigation confirmed that any attempts to limit or condition
         access to Seagen’s drugs would be defeated as customers would shift demand to
         alternatives. For example, in relation to cervical cancer, Keytruda, manufactured by
         MSD, competes with TIVDAK and is currently the standard of care for first line
         treatment of metastatic and recurrent cervical cancer.268 In any event, as set out
         above, any foreclosure strategy bundling Pfizer’s biosimilars with Seagen’s
         branded products would not be profitable given the price competition for biosimilar
         drugs.

(220)    In light of the lack of ability and incentive and on the basis of all available
         evidence, the Commission concludes that the Transaction does not raise serious
         doubts as to its compatibility with the internal market in this regard as a result of
         any conglomerate effects.
