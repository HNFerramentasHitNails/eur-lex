GENERAL COAL MARKET SITUATION FORECASTS FOR 1973

I. THE GENERAL ECONOMIC SITUATION - TRENDS IN INTERNAL ENERGY CONSUMPTION

Economic growth during the past year has differed markedly as between countries. While expansion continued to be vigorous in France throughout 1972, enabling the GNP to increase by 6 % compared with the previous year's level, the German Federal Republic and the Benelux countries saw a significant increase in economic activity in the first half year only to find this slowing down somewhat in the second half. The annual growth rate of the GNP between 1971 and 1972 has thus not differed materially from that recorded for 1970-71, being 3 % in the German Federal Republic, 3 75 % in the Netherlands and slightly over 4 % in Belgium. Economic expansion rates in Italy have not been fast moving either, where the GNP has been increasing by 3 % on average annually, despite a slight pick-up in market conditions during the year. Even this has not enabled any useful improvement in production capacities to take place.

In the three acceeding countries, the 1972 results have been scarcely any better, the GNP in the UK and Ireland having grown by 3 % and 4 75 % in Denmark.

The forecasts for 1973 outline a lively upsurge of market activity in the main industrialized countries both within and outside the Community, the latest market indications confirming this forecast. In the US the GNP should increase by 6 % between 1972 and 1973, ie at the same rate as for 1971 to 1972. A similar increase is forecast in Canada and a clear improvement is expected in Japan (10 %).

In the enlarged Community, the prospects of internal demand growing are improving. It is likely that the growth rate of management outlay will continue to be fast both in terms of value and volume. A recovery in investments is anticipated especially in Italy and the Netherlands, where a recession was seen in 1972.

Economic activity should increase at a greater pace, impelled especially by these two elements of demand but also due to outside expansion. The acceleration could be especially lively in the Federal Republic of Germany, where orders received have greatly increased in the last months of the year.

In France, the expansion of overall demand has been maintained and makes it possible to expect an accelerated expansion rate in 1973, especially in industrial expansion. Taking into account the anticipated strong economic expansion rate in Italy and, to a lesser extent in the Netherlands and the UEBL, and that the production in the acceding countries should also show an increased growth rate, the increase in the GNP of the enlarged Community in 1973 should reach some 5 %, and that of industrial production a little over 6 % on average per annum.

II. BREAKDOWN OF INTERNAL COAL DEMAND

1. TREND IN OVERALL INTERNAL DEMAND

The inclusion of new members, the UK primarily, affects the overall pattern of the coal market as shown in Tables 3 and 4. The consumption pattern in the United Kingdom is, in fact, different from that of the Community of Six. Power stations account for nearly half of total consumption and coking plants for a quarter, while in the Community of Six the situation is the reverse, carbonization accounting for more than half and power stations for 30 % of consumption.

The structure of coal consumption in the Community of Six has no intrinsic connection with the general level of industrial production. Carbonization depends on the balance between coke and the demand of the steel industry. With power stations, apart from variations in electricity demand, the policy on plant and conversion from one fuel to another has a significant effect on coal consumption.

Despite the economic recovery in 1972, which should foreseeably continue in 1973, coal consumption should recede and decrease from 180 to 160 million tons between 1971 and 1973, amounting to a reduction of 11 % in two years.

In the United Kingdom, overall demand for coal over two years will go down from 128 to 114 million tons, in other words also an 11 % (1) reduction, but the estimate for the main category, power stations, is unresolved until the detailed application of the Coal Industry Bill is known, according to which specific subsidies will be allotted to coal going to this consumption sector. In any event, all the Coal Industry Bill clauses can do is to consolidate the present position of coal.

(b) Steel industry

III. PRODUCTION OF METALLURGICAL COKE - COKING PLANT PRODUCTION CAPACITIES AND THEIR SUPPLY OF COAL

Overall metallurgical coke production for the Community of Six showed a regression in 1972 mainly due to the situation in Germany, where the need to adjust the balance in relation to accumulating coke stocks has proved of limited effect, coking plant activity being linked to fulfilling gas sale contracts. A slight regression was also noted in France. Forecasts for 1973 foresee activity picking up again, except in Germany. In the Netherlands production will increase by nearly a half, reaching 2 79 million tons as a result of new coking plants coming into operation.

Of the three new member countries only the United Kingdom possesses a carbonization industry. Ignoring the 1972 results affected by the strike, the forecasts for 1973 see a volume of production scarcely above that of 1971.

Total production for the Community of Nine should stand at 83 million tons, somewhat below the 1971 level. The coke balance will create new undistributed stocks of nearly 4 million tons in Germany and 1 75 million tons in the United Kingdom, where the undistributed stock level was very low at the beginning of the year.

V. EXTERNAL TRADE

1. Imports from third countries

World trade in coal was maintained in 1971 and 1972 at about 145 million tons, a level slightly lower than 1970, which was a peak in steel activity. The Community of Nine had a share of 22 % in this trade with 31 million tons of imports. Japan is still the largest importer, whose imports went up to 49 million tons in 1972 after a reduction in 1971, following the pattern of renewed activity in the steel industry. For the countries of Eastern Europe imports total 31 million tons, made up virtually entirely by movements within COMECON.

With exports on the world scale the continued reduction of deliveries from the United States at the expense of Australia and Canada is to be noted. The United States, with more than 50 million tons in 1972, still makes up 45 % of total exports to free economy countries. Australian exports reached 24 million tons in 1972, mostly consisting of long-term contracts with Japan. In its search to diversify its outlets, Australia is beginning to make inroads in the European market. Canada has experienced considerable difficulties in launching its exports, which have only got under way since 1970. Particular problems are those of recruitment, geological difficulties in mining and difficulties in coal preparation, with all that this entails for the financial aspect. Nevertheless, Canada is promoting the expansion of its exports, which in 1972 stood at nearly 8 million tons, consisting mainly of long-term contracts with Japan and also some trial cargoes for Europe.

Poland is raising its production potential fast and output has risen to 150 million tons in 1972 as against 124 million tons five years ago. Exports are above 30 million tons a year, over half of which is going to free economy countries. Coking coal plays a growing part in these figures with some 7 million tons.

The USSR, with its production of some 500 million tons, will maintain its exports at the reduced level of 24 million tons, 10 million tons of which will go outside the COMECON area.

In the United Kingdom, since the freeing of import restrictions in December 1970, the imports are not established ones but purchases intended to complement the supply to the two main consumers, the steel industry and power stations, and to a lesser extent the domestic sector. In 1972, purchases were affected by the coal strike and rose to 4 75 million tons. They should be reduced considerably in 1973, under 2 million tons, intended mainly for the steel industry with small tonnages for power stations.

The competitiveness of imported coal is still greatly influenced by variations in transport costs which, in certain cases, make up a considerable percentage of the ultimate delivered cost at a European port. Sea freights were at a particularly low level at the beginning of 1971 and remained so for a rather long period. The rates did not go up until September 1972, following extensive grain purchases by the USSR, among other causes. 2. Exports to third countries

In 1973 coal exports to third countries should reach 1 72 million tons, consisting mainly of German coal and of under 200 000 tons of United Kingdom coal (going to Ireland and Norway), whose main exports are directed towards the countries of the former Community.

German exports will remain at the reduced level of about 1 million tons, going mainly to Austria and Scandinavia.

Coke exports will rise to 2 75 million tons in 1973, following the pattern of previous years, 40 % of these being met by Germany and the remainder by the other Community countries including the United Kingdom. The destination of these tonnages is within Europe, mainly to Sweden, Switzerland and Austria.

VI. INTRA-COMMUNITY EXCHANGES

In view of the changes introduced into the trade pattern with the new member countries as from 1 January 1973, any examination of trade trends over the 3 years under review should distinguish between the Community of Six on the one hand and the United Kingdom on the other, being the only producer among the new members.

In the Community of Six, the general trend in coal trade continues to decline, 1972 being the year of a marked cut-back compared with the previous year, or 2 million tons less. The main reason for this downward movement lies in the reduced deliveries of coking coal from Germany to other Community countries due to the sharply competitive prices of imported coals with their unusually low freights throughout most of the past year. The amount involved is about 1 75 million tons and it primarily affects deliveries to coking plants in Belgium, Italy and the Netherlands. During 1973 the same trade pattern should be maintained at the levels of the previous year. As against this, lower deliveries are expected in other coal categories, especially in the Netherlands, due to production cut-backs.

In 1973 deliveries from the United Kingdom should reach a level close to that of 1971. This forecast contains a particular uncertainty factor, in that the trade now enjoys a freedom, which previously was up against quotas, as in Germany, and the granting of import licences, as in the majority of the Six Community countries. In any event, crossing the Channel, with breaking bulk twice and corresponding costs, represents a handicap for the extension of such trade.

Denmark receives only small tonnages from Community producers, while Ireland buys about 100 000 tons a year from the United Kingdom, overall imports being nearly one million tons.

90 % of metallurgical coke comes from Germany. The amount going to France and Luxembourg follows the demand pattern of the steel industry and should recover in 1973. Greatly reduced deliveries to the Netherlands can be noted due to the launching of new coking plants and, to a lesser extent, to Belgium, for the same reasons. Total intra-Community coke trade will fall below 8 million tons in 1973.

VII. PITHEAD COAL AND METALLURGICAL COKE STOCKS - TRENDS IN THE COAL BALANCE

The trend towards undistributed stocks started in 1971 continued in 1972 with further stocking of 3 77 million tons c.e. of coal and 3 75 million tons of coke, virtually all of it in Germany and only small amounts in Belgium and the Netherlands.

In the United Kingdom the February strike caused a temporary reduction in coal stocks, which returned to their original levels at the end of the year.

For 1973, the balance again shows a surplus in Germany with 4 78 million tons c.e. (coal) and 2 79 million tons coke and half a million tons of coal in France, where surplus availability would be wiped out if the production programme as planned were not fully achieved.

In the United Kingdom, the estimated balance gives a surplus availability of 14 million tons c.e. of coal, a figure based on minimum deliveries to power stations. Any additional tonnages supplied to this sector as a result of the recent subsidies by the Government would have to be subtracted from this first surplus estimate. In addition, it should be realized that the level of undistributed stocks was fairly low at the beginning of the year, when it amounted to no more than 16 days of production. The 1973 balance also shows a 1 75 million tons coke surplus, but undistributed stocks could not be replenished during the past year.

>PIC FILE= "T0012249"> CONCLUSIONS

The Community of The Nine includes the United Kingdom and Germany, both large-scale coal producers. As well as covering a considerable part of energy requirements, the coal industries of these two countries exhibit widely different features in terms of deliveries. In the United Kingdom, 98 % of output is consumed within the country while the German mining undertakings supply 22 % of their production to the other Community countries, more than two-thirds of this being made up of coking coal and coke for the steel industry.

All the coal industries of the Community have to face similar problems, in as much as labour-intensive industries have social problems and the geographical location of certain coalfields makes for regional problems. In addition, wage increases and equipment costs have been only partially compensated for by productivity increases over the years and to differing extents by coalfields. Nor have these costs been properly reflected in sale prices in the existing energy market conditions. This has resulted in a deteriorating financial situation of the undertakings and in the need for growing subsidies per ton.

Internally, the enlargement of the Community should not bring rapid or profound changes in the trade pattern, though the development of exchanges between the United Kingdom and the Continent should not be ruled out, despite the transport costs caused by crossing the Channel. On the other hand, if the pound sterling should remain at its lower rate, this could only facilitate the disposal of English coal.

In imports from third countries diversification continues, the total remaining unchanged. Deliveries from the United States are being reduced, their place being filled by other sources of supply : Poland, Australia and, to a lesser extent, Canada and South Africa. Increasing purchases in Poland, and the fact that Denmark is obtaining virtually all its coal supplies from East European countries, mean that half the Community's imports now come from these countries.

The delivered costs of coal imported from distant sources depend on maritime transport costs to a large extent. The recent increases in sea freight rates, both actual freights and time charters, which had admittedly reached abnormally low levels, can only make the price of these coals very much dearer.

The balance for 1973 shows a surplus of stocks in the two large producing countries : 9 million tons c.e. (coal, and coke converted to c.e.) in Germany and 10 to 15 million tons c.e. in the United Kingdom. According to our working hypothesis, total undistributed stocks at the end of the year will reach 30 million tons c.e. in Germany and 20 to 25 million tons c.e. in the United Kingdom ; these figures should relate to the level of production, under 100 million tons in Germany and 140 million tons in the United Kingdom, to give them their proper significance. In the other coal-producing countries availability and requirements are balancing out.

Any approach to the coal problem assumes options and choices among well-known elements of the problem but these should be weighted in an appropriate way. Above all it is essential in this study not to view the European coal system in isolation but to place it in its general European energy context, with due regard to the rapid advances in the world's energy supply structures.
