SPECIAL REPORT OF THE COURT OF AUDITORS on sales of reduced-price butter within the Community This report was adopted by the Court of Auditors at its meeting of 18 March 1982, in application of Article 206a (4) of the EEC Treaty. It had previously been sent on 23 November 1981 to the Commission for its comments, which are reproduced in the Annex. 13 April 1982

>PIC FILE= "T0035333"> >PIC FILE= "T0035334"> 1. INTRODUCTION

1.1. The existence of large butter surpluses is a persistent feature in the Community. They were until recently constantly on the increase, due to a continuing growth in production on the one hand (some 1 75 % per annum) and a continuing decline in per capita consumption on the other (7 72 kg in 1969 against 6 75 kg in 1979). An analysis of the underlying causes of these trends lies outside the scope of this report.

1.2. The problems raised by the definition of a storage policy are also not dealt with in this report. The fact is that in a situation of permanent over-production, storage is not a suitable means of reestablishing a balance between supply and demand. On the contrary, it serves to increase the associated economic and financial costs. Only where there is some hope that over-production will be temporary is storage acceptable. It then acts as a regulatory mechanism, rather than as a means of merely postponing consequences.

1.3. The problem is one of finding the most effective and least expensive means of disposing of the surpluses, whether they constitute direct pressure on the market or whether they are stored for some time. At an early stage, the Community had to make provision for mechanisms designed firstly to clear the intervention stocks of quantities of butter which could not be normally disposed of during a milk year, then to prevent or re-absorb surpluses which were tending to become structural.

The present report concerns this whole system of mechanisms. It has three aims: (1) to provide a comprehensive list of the measures involved;

(2) to assess the conditions under which they are managed and audited, on the basis of information obtained from the Commission and on-the-spot visits in the Member States;

(3) to assess their effectiveness by comparing their measurable effects, essentially on consumption, with their unit cost, in order to determine the cost of the additional consumption brought about, or the marginal cost. This evaluation of effectiveness includes comparison with the cost of exports made with the aid of refunds either on the world market or as food aid.

1.4. The Court has already examined the application of some of the measures to aid disposal of milk products, and butter in particular : in 1978 the so-called "Christmas butter" operation and in 1979 the supply of butter on special terms to armed forces (1).

1.5. (a) Disposal of butter at a reduced price is understood to mean any sale of butter at a price lower than the intervention price, keeping in mind that the latter, which is fixed annually, represents the price of entry into public storage and therefore, normally, the floor price on the market.

(a) In order to manage the butter market, the milk products division has information from various sources at its disposal. Regulation (EEC) No 210/69 of 31 January 1969 (1) on communications between Member States and the Commission in respect of milk and milk products specifies the information to be provided to the Commission by the Member States. The Commission also follows butter prices on the world market, butter production, the prices of competitive oils and fats and the position of securities which have not been released.

Finally, it has available information received through the Management Committee and various professional organizations.

This information should in theory enable the Commission to manage stocks, to adjust the level of aid or reductions where necessary and to prevent speculation.

(b) It should be noted that the Commission does not have first-hand information and that as a general rule the information in its possession is not up-to-date. The decisions which it has to take are necessarily late as the information in its possession does not allow for an accurate and rapid observation of trends. The disadvantages of this situation are accentuated the more the management seeks to be direct, as is the case with the tendering procedure. Moreover, the Commission does not properly follow up the implementation of the statutory rules, particularly with regard to audit (cf. paragraph 3.2 below) and it does not have any general system of evaluating the results obtained by the various measures. 5. SPECIFIC EFFECT ON CONSUMPTION

5.1. WEAKNESS IN THE SYSTEM OF ASSESSMENT

The general objective of the measures based on Regulation (EEC) No 804/68 is, it will be recalled, to dispose of the surpluses held in storage and to prevent the creation of new surpluses. The expansion of the internal market, i.e. the increase of direct and indirect butter consumption in Community countries by means of reducing the selling price, thus represents one possible way of achieving this objective. Given the diversity and cost of the measures analyzed above, it would be of use to be able to evaluate the results individually rather than on an overall basis.

A system of regular assessment, set up and supervised by the Commission, would appear to constitute a normal method of rational management. No such system exists, however, as already noted by the Court and acknowledged by the Commission in the special report on the 1977/78 "Christmas butter" operation. The Commission is certainly interested in data for assessing the effectiveness, in terms of additional consumption, of the measures which it proposes or prescribes, but hitherto, this has only been possible on the basis of the data supplied by institutions or national organizations which must be presumed reliable in themselves, but which cannot offer equally satisfactory guarantees as regards the comparability of the methods used.

The Court has had no option but to use these same data, after checking the sources, and has endeavoured to derive its own interpretation from the information.

5.2. DEFINITIONS : ELASTICITY, SUBSTITUTION AND EFFECTIVENESS

(a) If it is supposed that the quantity of butter bought varies in accordance with the price at a given moment, the amount of variation will be measured by the price elasticity of demand. The elasticity coefficient shows the demand which can be expected from a 1 % change in the price, all other things being equal.

Usually the coefficient is preceded by a minus sign, to indicate that when prices increase, purchases fall : if a coefficient were found of - 0 750 in a given Member State, it would mean that, in percentage terms, the quantity consumed decreases half as quickly as the price increases.

In the opposite case then, a fall in price will result in an increase in consumption of the same magnitude, shown by a plus sign preceding the coefficient.

(b) The quantitative effect on purchases of the reduction in price can be calculated by the use of such elasticity coefficients.

Thus, for a given type of sale at a reduced price, with a given level of aid, this effect may be measured: 1. either by the substitution rate n : 100 kg disposed of with aid are substituted for n kg sold in the normal way without aid;

2. or by the rate of effectiveness n' : out of 100 kg disposed of with aid, n' kg are sold in addition to those which would have been sold in the normal way without aid.

The arithmetical relationship between the two rates can be expressed as : n + n' = 100 (1).

(b) the ice-cream market ist partially a captive market because of the rule in certain countries that a certain proportion of butter must be incorporated in the manufacture of ice-cream (Federal Republic of Germany, Denmark, France);

(c) the effectiveness of the end-of-year measures has shown a tendency to decline ; it was estimated at 30 % for 1977/78, and fell to 25 % for 1978/79. This trend, which stabilized in 1979/80 at around 27 %, conceals significant differences between the five countries concerned and according to the type of measure;

(d) the relatively low effectiveness of social aid, limited to Ireland (20 %), may be explained by the already high level of consumption and of general aid to consumption;

(e) the lowest rate of effectiveness is that of the general aid to consumption. In the United Kingdom, price elasticity of demand varies between - 0 737 and - 0 747, depending upon the source, and, taking into account price reductions on the normal market, i.e. 16 to 17 %, the rate of effectiveness in 1979 was in the region of 7 % and on the decline;

(f) on the basis of the rates of effectiveness adopted by the Commission (calculated from data supplied by the Member States), all other things being equal, an additional 196 000 tonnes of butter was disposed of in 1979 as a result of aids and price reductions. Without these measures, the level of internal consumption would have been around 1 500 000 tonnes only, instead of 1 697 000 tonnes. Consumption was thus 13 % higher than what it would have been had normal prices applied. The effectiveness of the measures is, however, gradually declining, as special management measures adapted to a certain type of market are being superseded by general and permanent measures which favour the consumer rather than consumption.

6.2.2. Exports

As already stated in the introduction to the report (paragraph 1.3.3), any measure of the effectiveness of the disposal measures on the internal market involves comparison with the cost of the exports made with the aid of Community refunds.

In terms of unit and global costs, this comparison was made above in paragraph 6.1, and it is not especially favourable to exports, despite a marked tendency towards a fall in refund rates in 1980.

The situation is completely different if the marginal costs are considered, i.e. the cost of the additional consumption brought about by the aid measures : it may be assumed, in fact, that every export with the aid of refunds represents an additional quantity sold and may be assigned a physical effectiveness rate of 100, while only a part, and sometimes a very small part, of each internal disposal measure represents additional consumption.

Therefore, if storage costs are not taken into account, only internal measures such as aid for consumption by armed forces and organizations, of limited application in any case, appear less expensive than export. If, on the contrary, storage costs are taken into account and given that the vast majority of exports come directly from the market, external sales appear as cheaper by far than any of the internal measures.

This conclusion must be qualified in one respect only : the absorption capacity of the external market. It is not certain whether, in 1979 or 1980, the latter would have absorbed, at the same cost, the 200 000 or so additional tonnes that would have been available in the absence of any internal measure. The conclusion nevertheless remains that it was proper, in terms of sound financial management, to favour exports in the situation prevailing.

7. GENERAL CONCLUSIONS

From the preceding observations a number of general conclusions may be drawn for all of the measures for the disposal of butter at reduced prices examined in this report. (a) The objective of the regulations which are still today the basis for the various measures was the disposal of Community butter production, which had outstripped the needs of the internal market, by encouraging additional consumption : this objective has not been achieved, since consumption has continued to decline. The measures, however, have until recently proliferated and their cost has increased considerably, as if the initial objective was gradually but surreptitiously being replaced by another : that of slowing down the increase in the price of butter for the benefit of the Community consumer. Such e development today requires a clear choice and thus, where necessary, a revision of the basic legislation, if only because any assessment of the measures'effectiveness presupposes, from the outset, a specific objective.

(b) Whatever the objective pursued, it is absolutely essential that the Commission adopt a permanent and reliable system of measuring the results obtained : this is not, at present, the case, since the persons responsible for management assemble the data necessary for their decisions or proposals solely from information provided, with some delay, by the Member States themselves, and with no guarantee as to its homogeneity or objectivity.

(c) With regard to the officially declared objective and in so far as information on effectiveness is nevertheless available and accepted in Community discussions, there appears to be considerable variation in the marginal cost of the different measures, i.e. of the cost per additional quantity marketed.

(d) A rational management of the measures implies not only an assessment of their economic and financial effectiveness but also a strict appraisal of the actual circumstances in which they are implemented, in particular to ensure observance of the legal conditions and to prevent the butter being used for a purpose other than that intended by the regulations.

ANNEX III SALE OF BUTTER FROM PUBLIC STOCKS AT A HIGHER PRICE (Normal sale)

This measure is used by operators who are obliged to meet short-term contracts for a certain quality of butter which they do not have available.

Regulation (EEC) No 2315/76 allows for the intervention agencies in this event to sell butter in public storage under the following conditions: - sale price equal to the purchase price applied by the intervention agency on the same day, plus a fixed sum of 2 75 u.a. (3 702 ECU)/100 kg. In 1973 and 1974 this amount was 3 u.a. (3 762 ECU)/100 kg,

- the butter disposed of must have been in storage for at least six months.

The 2 75 u.a. (3 702 ECU)/100 kg covers only a small part of the storage expenses incurred by the intervention agency for the butter marketed for at least six months.
